## Why this recipe

Because the evaporation budget in `heat.saute` is an abstraction until you watch a
pan fail.

Most vegetables give you enough slack to get away with crowding. They release
water slowly enough that a hot pan can mostly keep up, and the result is
disappointing rather than obviously wrong.

Mushrooms have no slack. They are around ninety percent water, and they release
most of it in the first few minutes of heating. Crowd them and the pan floods
visibly — you can watch the liquid pool, watch the sizzle die, and watch the
mushrooms turn grey and squeak. Give them room and they brown deeply in the same
number of minutes.

Same mushrooms, same pan, same heat, same fat. The only variable is how many were
in the pan at once, and the difference is the loudest demonstration of a general
principle you will get.

## The budget, made concrete

A pound of mushrooms holds roughly 400 grams of water.

A 12-inch skillet at medium-high can evaporate somewhere in the region of 20 to
30 grams of water per minute, and only from **exposed hot metal** — the area not
covered by food.

Cover the whole pan and you have very little exposed metal, so evaporation
collapses while water release stays high. The pan floods, its temperature drops
to 212F and pins there, and browning is now off the table until every drop is
gone. By the time the liquid finally boils away, the mushrooms have been stewing
in it for ten minutes and have gone rubbery.

Cover two thirds and evaporation keeps pace. Water leaves as fast as it arrives,
the surface stays dry, the temperature climbs past 300F, and you brown.

Two batches of six minutes beats one batch of eighteen, and it beats it on time as
well as on quality.

## The dry-pan method

Worth knowing, and it is genuinely counterintuitive.

Put the mushrooms in a **completely dry pan** — no fat at all — over medium-high
heat. They will squeak, release all their water, and simmer in it. Keep going
until every drop has boiled off, which takes five to eight minutes. Only then add
the fat, and brown.

This works because the water was always going to come out. Adding fat at the start
means that fat spends the whole watery phase sitting there getting nothing done,
and it means the water is now suspended in fat rather than freely evaporating.
Wait, and the fat arrives when it can actually do its job.

Two advantages: you can cook the full pound at once, and you use less fat. One
disadvantage: no browning happens during the first phase, so the total time is
similar.

Try both. The dry-pan method is more reliable; the two-batch method browns a
little more deeply.

## Why salt goes last

Salt draws water out of cells by osmosis. That is useful when you want water out
— it is the mechanism behind dry brining and behind sweating onions.

Here it is precisely wrong. You are trying to get the surface dry. Salting at the
start pulls a fresh supply of water to the surface at exactly the moment you are
fighting to remove it, and it pushes a pan that was marginal over into flooding.

Salt after browning. Every time.

## Why the butter waits

Butter is around 80 percent fat, 16 percent water, and the rest milk solids and
protein.

Those milk solids brown — that is what browned butter is — and then they burn,
well below the temperature you want for the high-heat browning phase. Start with
butter and you will have black speckles and an acrid smell before the mushrooms
have colored.

Neutral oil for the heat, butter for the flavor at the end. This is a general
pattern worth carrying: **cook in the fat that tolerates the temperature, finish
in the fat that tastes good.** See `heat.fat-as-medium` and `flavor.fat`.

## Why the acid at the end

Mushrooms are savory and earthy. Butter is rich. Together they are heavy, and
heavy dishes read as flat even when they are correctly salted.

A teaspoon of sherry vinegar off the heat cuts through and makes the whole thing
taste finished rather than merely rich. It is the same correction as the final
acid in a pan sauce, and it is the single most common omission in home vegetable
cooking. See `flavor.acid`.

## Variations

- **With shallot**: a minced shallot with the butter, before the garlic.
- **Deglazed**: a splash of white wine, sherry, or Madeira after the browning,
  scraped and reduced. Now it is closer to a pan sauce.
- **With cream**: 1/4 cup of heavy cream after the butter, reduced briefly. Serve
  on toast.
- **Mixed wild mushrooms**: cook them separately by type. They hold very different
  amounts of water and will not finish together.
- **On toast with an egg**: the whole thing on grilled sourdough with a fried egg
  is a complete meal.
- **Duxelles**: chop finely instead of quartering, cook down with shallot until
  almost dry and paste-like. A component rather than a side.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grey, squeaky, wet | Crowded | Two batches, two thirds coverage |
| Liquid pooled in the pan | Water outran evaporation | Pull half out, raise the heat, let it recover |
| Never browned even uncrowded | Salted at the start | Salt at the end |
| Beige rather than golden | Pan not hot enough, or stirred too much | Hotter pan, leave them alone 3-4 minutes |
| Stuck and tore apart | Moved before the crust formed | Wait. Browned food releases itself |
| Black speckles, acrid smell | Butter used for the high-heat phase | Neutral oil first, butter last |
| Rubbery | Stewed in their own liquid for too long | This is the crowded-pan failure, arriving late |
| Bitter | Burnt garlic | Garlic goes in with the butter, at the end |
| Tastes heavy and flat | No acid | Vinegar or lemon, off the heat |
| Soggy from the start | Soaked in water and not dried | Wipe or rinse quickly, then dry thoroughly |
