## Why this recipe

Because the reason home aromatic bases are under-cooked is not ignorance. It is
that it is seven o'clock and you are hungry.

Every source tells you to cook the onion until softened, about five minutes.
Five minutes gets you a warm onion that still tastes raw, and that rawness stays
in the finished dish permanently — see `prep.aromatic-bases`. Doing it properly
takes twenty-five to thirty-five minutes, and on a weeknight nobody has that, so
everybody rushes it, and a large fraction of home cooking carries a faint harsh
note that nobody can name.

The fix is not discipline. It is **moving the step to a different day.**

An hour on a Sunday produces twelve properly-cooked bases. A Tuesday braise now
starts twenty-five minutes ahead, with a base that was cooked without time
pressure by someone who was not hungry.

This single habit improves weeknight cooking more than any technique in the first
two slices, because it does not ask you to cook better under pressure. It removes
the pressure.

## The ratio

Classic mirepoix is **2 : 1 : 1 by weight** — onion, carrot, celery. Not by piece
count, which is why the ingredient list is in grams. A large onion and a small
carrot are not one-to-one anything.

Vary it deliberately:

| Base | Ratio | Fat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mirepoix | 2 onion : 1 carrot : 1 celery | Butter or oil |
| White mirepoix | Onion, celery, leek, parsnip — no carrot | Butter |
| Soffritto | 2 : 1 : 1, cut finer | Olive oil |
| The trinity | 2 onion : 1 celery : 1 green bell pepper | Oil, butter, or lard |
| Suppengrun | Leek, carrot, celeriac, roughly equal | Butter |

White mirepoix exists because carrot adds orange color and noticeable sweetness,
which is wrong for a pale sauce or a delicate stock. Parsnip substitutes the body
without the color.

## Why sweat and not brown

The most important decision in the batch, and it is about optionality.

A **sweated** base is neutral. Clean, sweet, and it does not push a dish in any
direction, which means it works under a bright tomato sauce, a chicken braise, a
vegetable soup, or a risotto.

A **browned** base is deep and assertive and it has already decided. It is right
under a beef braise and wrong under a fish stew.

You are freezing twelve portions for dishes you have not chosen yet. Keep them
neutral. If a particular dish wants depth, you can brown the base further in the
pan after thawing — the frozen cubes go straight into hot fat and will happily
take on color. **You can always add browning later. You cannot remove it.**

## Using it

Straight from the freezer into a hot pan. No thawing.

The cubes will release their water first and steam for a minute or two, then the
water will boil off and they will start frying in their own oil. At that point
proceed with whatever you are making.

If the dish wants a browned base, keep going another five to eight minutes after
the water is gone.

- **Tomato sauce**: cubes in, bloom tomato paste, add tomatoes. See
  `rigatoni-soffritto-tomato`.
- **Braise**: sear the meat, remove it, cubes into the fat, deglaze, return the
  meat.
- **Soup**: cubes in, stock in, done.
- **Risotto**: cubes in, rice in, toast, then stock.
- **Beans**: cubes into the pot with the beans from the start.
- **Ragu**: brown the cubes properly first, then the meat.

## Scaling and honesty about the work

The cutting is most of it. Thirty to forty minutes with a knife, and no amount of
technique makes 2 kg of vegetables fast.

A food processor will do it in three minutes, and the result is worse — it bruises
and tears rather than cutting, releasing more water and more pungency, and it
produces wildly uneven pieces. For a base that will be cooked to collapse and then
frozen, that is a more defensible tradeoff than usual. If the choice is between
processor-cut base and no base, use the processor.

Cutting by hand is also, straightforwardly, the practice session for
`knife.uniform-cuts`. Two thousand grams of vegetables is a lot of repetitions,
and repetitions are how the grip stops being something you think about.

## Keeping

- **Freezer**: 3 months easily, 6 with some quality loss. Label with the date.
- **Fridge**: 5 days in an airtight container, oil covering the surface.
- **Do not can it.** Low-acid vegetables in oil are a botulism risk without
  pressure canning, and this is not the place to improvise. Freeze it.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Still tastes sharp | Under-sweated | It will not cook out later. Longer next time |
| Browned when you wanted neutral | Heat too high | Medium-low, and stir more often |
| Stewed rather than sweated | Pan crowded | Wider pan, or two batches |
| Crunchy carrot in a soft base | Uneven dice, or carrot added late | Even cuts, plenty of time |
| Watery on thawing | Frozen while still warm | Cool completely, spread on a sheet pan |
| Freezer-burnt | Poor packaging | Solid-freeze first, then bag with the air pressed out |
| Used the wrong base in a dish | Not labelled | They look identical frozen. Label them |
| Not enough flavor in the finished dish | Not enough fat in the base | The fat is the carrier. Be generous |
| Tastes stale after a few months | Kept too long | Three months is the comfortable window |
