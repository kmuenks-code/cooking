## Why this recipe

Because tomato pasta is the dish people believe they already know how to make,
and it is the one where the gap between adequate and good is entirely technique.

There is nothing expensive here and nothing hard to find. A can of tomatoes, three
vegetables, a box of pasta. Every difference between this and a mediocre version
of the same ingredients comes from decisions: how evenly the base was cut, how
long it was sweated, whether the paste was bloomed, how long the sauce cooked and
whether that was chosen or accidental, and whether the pasta was finished in the
pan or dumped into a bowl.

Six decisions. No ingredient rescues a wrong answer to any of them.

## The sequence, memorized as a shape

Forget the quantities. Remember this:

```
dice even  →  sweat the base slow  →  garlic late
           →  bloom the paste  →  crush tomatoes in
           →  simmer to a chosen time
           →  pasta out early  →  finish in the pan with its water
           →  off heat: basil, raw oil, salt last
```

That shape is not specific to tomato. Swap the base to onion-ginger-garlic and it
is a curry. Swap the tomatoes for stock and cream and it is a different pasta
entirely. Once you have the shape, the recipe stops being something you look up.

## Why the base takes twenty minutes

Because the sharpness of raw onion does not cook out later.

The volatile sulfur compounds that make cut allium taste harsh — see
`flavor.aromatics-alliums` — leave during the slow gentle cooking at the start, and
only then. Under-sweat the base and that harshness is still in the sauce forty-
five minutes later, and it will still be there tomorrow. The dish tastes subtly
off and nobody can say why.

Recipes that say "cook the onion until softened, about 5 minutes" are describing
a warm onion, not a sweated one. Give it the time.

## Why the tomato paste gets bloomed

Tomato paste out of the tube has a flat, slightly metallic note and its sugars are
unavailable. Thirty to sixty seconds in hot fat caramelizes those sugars and
drives the raw note off. The color change from bright red to brick is your cue.

It is the highest return-per-second step in the recipe, and it is the one most
often skipped because it looks like nothing is happening.

## Why you taste at fifteen, thirty, and forty-five

The single most useful thing in this recipe is the discovery that **you are
cooking three different sauces and choosing one.**

Most home tomato sauce is cooked for however long it took the pasta to boil. That
is not a decision. Once you have tasted the same pot at three points you will
never again cook a tomato sauce without knowing what you are aiming at.

For this dish, thirty to forty-five is the sweet spot. For a summer pasta with
good tomatoes, fifteen. For a lasagne, ninety.

## Why the pasta comes out early

Pasta cooked through has fully gelatinized starch all the way in and has absorbed
all the water it can. Put that in a pan and it absorbs nothing more — it just
softens toward mush.

Pasta pulled three minutes early still has a firm core. In the pan it finishes
cooking by absorbing **sauce** rather than water, which seasons it all the way
through instead of only on the surface, and it thickens the sauce as it does.

That is why the timing is not fussiness. It is what makes the finish work at all.

## Why the pasta water matters

The cloudy water is a working emulsifier, not waste.

The sauce has a lot of olive oil in it and a lot of water from the tomatoes, and
those two want to separate. Dissolved starch from the pasta positions itself at
the boundaries between fat droplets and water and stops them from merging. Add
hard agitation — which is why you toss rather than stir gently — and you get a
real emulsion.

The visible result is the sauce turning glossy and gripping the pasta instead of
sliding off it. Once you have seen that transition once you will look for it
every time, and you will notice its absence in restaurants.

## Variations

- **Arrabbiata**: skip the carrot and celery, double the garlic, bloom a heavy
  pinch of chile flakes in the oil at the start.
- **Amatriciana**: render 4 oz of diced guanciale or pancetta first and build the
  base in that fat. Pecorino instead of parmesan.
- **Puttanesca**: melt three anchovies into the oil with the garlic, add olives
  and capers with the tomatoes. Never sugar.
- **With sausage**: brown crumbled sausage first, remove, build the base in the
  fat, return it with the tomatoes.
- **Vodka**: a splash of vodka after blooming the paste, and 1/2 cup cream at the
  end. The alcohol carries aroma compounds that neither fat nor water can.
- **Summer version**: 2 lbs of genuinely ripe fresh tomatoes, peeled, and cook
  only 15 to 20 minutes. Not worth doing outside of August.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sauce pooled under the pasta | Sauced in a bowl, or not enough agitation | Finish in the pan. Toss hard, with water and heat |
| Sauce broke, oil floating | Not enough starch or not enough movement | More pasta water, toss harder |
| Too acidic | Under-cooked, under-salted, or not enough fat | Longer, more salt, more oil. Sugar last and sparingly |
| Tastes of can | Paste not bloomed | Thirty seconds next time |
| Bitter | Burnt garlic, or scorched on the bottom | Not fixable. Garlic later, bare simmer, scrape the bottom |
| Raw onion taste underneath | Base under-sweated | It will not cook out later. Twenty minutes next time |
| Crunchy carrot in a soft sauce | Uneven dice, or carrot added too late | Even cuts, and carrot goes in with plenty of time |
| Pasta bland | Water not salted | 10-20g per liter. The only chance |
| Pasta mushy | Cooked through before it hit the pan | Three minutes early |
| Tomatoes never broke down | Diced tomatoes, not whole peeled | The firming agent in diced cans prevents it |
| Parmesan went stringy | Grated over direct heat | Off the heat, always |
| Basil black and bitter | Added while cooking | Torn in off the heat |
| Sauce too salty at the end | Salted before reducing | Salt lightly at the start, correct at the very end |
