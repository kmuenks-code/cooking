## Why this recipe

Every other pasta dish gives you somewhere to hide. If the emulsion does not form
in a tomato sauce, you still have tomato sauce — thinner and less glossy than it
should be, but recognizably dinner.

Cacio e pepe has no sauce underneath the emulsion. The emulsion **is** the sauce.
Cheese, starchy water, and pepper, held together by physics. Get it wrong and you
are looking at naked spaghetti with a rubbery lump of pecorino welded to the
tongs, and there is no pretending otherwise.

That is exactly why it is worth cooking as the graduation exercise for
`prep.pasta`. It gives you unambiguous feedback.

## What is actually happening

Pecorino is roughly a third fat, a third protein, and a third water. Melt it into
a liquid and you are trying to suspend that fat as droplets in water — an
emulsion, and an unstable one, because the cheese proteins would much rather bond
to each other than stay dispersed.

Two things keep them apart.

**Dissolved starch** from the pasta water. Long amylose and amylopectin molecules
position themselves at the fat-water boundaries and physically block the droplets
from merging. This is the same mechanism as any pan-finished pasta, just with far
less margin.

**Temperature control.** Above roughly 165F, casein proteins in the cheese
denature, contract, and squeeze their fat out — the clump. Below that, they stay
dispersed. The window is real and it is narrow.

So the two failure modes have two different causes: **not enough starch** gives
you a thin, watery sauce that never comes together, and **too much heat** gives
you the clump. They look different and they are fixed differently.

## Why less water

Counterintuitive, and it is the most important departure from normal pasta
practice.

Normally you boil pasta in a lot of water so it moves freely and the temperature
recovers fast. Here you want the opposite: **the water is an ingredient**, and its
value is its starch concentration.

Cook 200g of pasta in four liters and the water stays thin. Cook it in one liter
and it goes genuinely viscous. That viscous water is what carries the emulsion.

Some people go further and cook the pasta risotto-style — a small amount of water,
added gradually — which produces the most concentrated starch of all. Worth trying
once you have the standard method working.

## Why pecorino specifically

Pecorino romano is a sheep's-milk cheese, aged, hard, and very salty. It is also
harder to emulsify than parmesan, because it is drier and its proteins are more
tightly bound.

Traditional Roman practice is all pecorino. If it keeps breaking on you, cutting
it with parmesan — say 70/30 — makes it substantially more forgiving, because
parmigiano reggiano melts more readily. Purists will object. It is a reasonable
place to start and you can move toward all-pecorino as your technique improves.

What will not work is pre-grated cheese from a bag. It is coated with cellulose or
potato starch to stop it caking, and that coating prevents clean melting. Grate it
yourself.

## Why toast and crush the pepper

Black pepper's aroma comes largely from volatile terpenes that sit in the outer
layer of the peppercorn, and its pungency from piperine. Both degrade once ground —
pre-ground pepper has lost most of its aromatic character and is left with dusty
heat.

Toasting for a minute drives the volatiles to the surface. Crushing coarsely
rather than grinding fine gives you pockets of pepper you actually taste, rather
than a uniform background burn.

Two teaspoons sounds like a lot. It is not — pepper is one of the two named
ingredients.

## Variations

- **Cacio e pepe with parmesan cut in**: 70 pecorino, 30 parmigiano. Easier, still
  good.
- **Gricia**: render diced guanciale first, build the sauce in its fat. This is
  cacio e pepe plus pork, and the fat makes the emulsion notably more stable.
- **Carbonara**: gricia plus egg yolk, off the heat. The yolk is an additional
  emulsifier, which is why carbonara is more forgiving than cacio e pepe despite
  its reputation.
- **Amatriciana**: gricia plus tomato.
- **With butter as insurance**: a tablespoon of cold butter tossed in before the
  cheese adds fat and phospholipids that stabilize the emulsion. Not traditional,
  and it works.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cheese clumped into a rubbery lump | Pan too hot | Off the heat, wait 30 seconds. Never over direct flame |
| Cheese clumped, pan was cool | Cheese was cold, or grated too coarsely | Room temperature, fine grate |
| Sauce thin and watery | Not enough starch | Less cooking water next time. More reserved water now |
| Sauce never came together | All the cheese at once | Add in three additions, tossing between |
| Grainy rather than smooth | Partial seizing, or not enough agitation | More water, keep tossing. It can sometimes be brought back |
| Too salty | Water salted normally | 5g per liter. The pecorino brings the rest |
| Pepper tastes dusty and flat | Pre-ground | Whole peppercorns, toasted, coarsely crushed |
| Ran out of pasta water | Everyone does, once | Reserve two mugs. You will use more than you think |
| Seized as it sat | The emulsion is temporary | Warm bowls, serve immediately, no exceptions |
| Cheese would not melt at all | Pre-grated with anti-caking coating | Grate it yourself |
