## Why this recipe

Because a spoon is an unforgiving instrument.

On a plate you eat a dish selectively. You can take a bit of this and a bit of
that, and an over-browned fragment sitting next to an under-cooked chunk is
something you notice but do not really confront.

A soup arrives one spoonful at a time, and every spoonful is a random sample of
the pot. If your dice was uneven, roughly every third spoonful contains something
that went too far and something that did not go far enough, together, and you
cannot avoid either.

That is why minestrone is the right graduation exercise for `knife.uniform-cuts`.
Eight vegetables, one target size, and immediate feedback.

## Size and time are different variables

The lesson people usually take from "uniform cuts" is *make everything the same
size*, and then they put everything in the pot at once and wonder why the
zucchini dissolved while the potato was still hard.

Uniformity works **within** an ingredient. It is what stops one carrot cube from
scorching while its neighbor stays raw.

**Across** ingredients, density and water content decide timing, and identical
size does not make identical cooking rate. A quarter-inch potato cube and a
quarter-inch zucchini cube are not comparable at all.

So you have two separate controls:

- **Same size within an ingredient** → one outcome instead of a distribution.
- **Different entry times across ingredients** → everything finishes together.

This recipe uses both, and that combination is the actual skill.

| Goes in | When | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onion, carrot, celery | First, as the base | They need the slow cook, not just the heat |
| Potato | With the liquid | Densest. 30 minutes |
| Green beans, cabbage | 20 min in | Middling. 10 minutes |
| Zucchini, cooked beans | 8 min from the end | Mostly water. Will vanish if early |
| Herbs, raw oil | Off the heat | Volatile. Heat destroys them |

## The parmesan rind

The most cost-effective ingredient in this curriculum, and it is normally thrown
away.

A parmesan rind is the dried, hardened outer surface of a wheel that has aged for
a year or more. That ageing broke down protein into free glutamate — and the same
process produced nucleotides. Both umami families in one piece of what is
otherwise waste. See `flavor.umami` for why having both matters so much more than
having a lot of one.

Dropped into a simmering pot for an hour, it releases that slowly into the broth
and softens without dissolving. Fish it out at the end, or dice it and leave it in
for whoever finds it.

Keep every rind you generate in a bag in the freezer. This soup is largely water
and vegetables, and without the rind it tastes like it.

**No rind?** In rough order of preference: a melted anchovy in the base, a
tablespoon of white miso whisked in at the end off the heat, a splash of soy
sauce, or a couple of dried shiitake dropped in with the liquid.

## Why water rather than stock

Traditional minestrone is made with water, and it is not a compromise.

The soup is built from an aromatic base cooked properly, tomato, a parmesan rind,
and eight vegetables simmering for forty minutes. Those things make a broth. Using
a commercial chicken stock on top of that adds a competing flavor and, usually, a
lot of salt you did not control.

If you have a genuinely good homemade stock, use it. If your alternative is a
carton, use water and the rind.

## Variations

- **Minestrone al pesto (soupe au pistou)**: a spoonful of pesto stirred into each
  bowl at the table. Excellent.
- **With pasta**: a handful of ditalini in for the last 8 minutes. Note it will
  keep absorbing liquid overnight and the leftovers will be a stew.
- **With rice**: same, in for the last 15.
- **Ribollita**: much thicker, with stale bread stirred in until it collapses.
  Tuscan, and arguably better than the original.
- **Spring**: asparagus, peas, and fava beans instead of the winter vegetables.
  Shorter cook, 20 minutes total.
- **With pancetta**: render 3 oz diced at the start and build the base in the fat.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Some vegetables mush, some raw | Uneven cuts, or all in at once | Even within each, staggered across |
| Zucchini disappeared | Added too early | Last 8 minutes |
| Broth cloudy, everything shredded | Boiled instead of simmered | Bare simmer. Same temperature, less violence |
| Tastes thin and watery | No rind, no bloomed paste | Both. They are most of the flavor |
| Flat despite plenty of salt | Missing umami, not salt | Rind, anchovy, or miso |
| Raw onion note underneath | Base under-sweated | 15 minutes minimum. It will not cook out |
| Potato dissolved into cloud | Boiled, or cut too small | Simmer, and a full quarter inch |
| Bland vegetables in a seasoned broth | Salted only at the end | Salt lightly at each stage |
| Herbs went dark and bitter | Added during the simmer | Off the heat, torn |
| Leftovers turned into stew | Pasta or rice kept absorbing | Cook the starch separately and add per bowl |
