## Why this recipe

Two mechanisms, both invisible in most cooking, both unmissable here.

**Fat as a solvent.** Most of what makes garlic and chile taste like themselves
is fat-soluble, not water-soluble. You can watch the extraction happen: the oil
gradually takes on color and smell, and by the end it tastes profoundly of
garlic while the garlic slices themselves are mild. The flavor moved.

**Starch as an emulsifier.** Pasta water is not just salty water. It is loaded
with starch that leached out of the pasta, and starch molecules stabilize the
boundary between oil and water. Toss oil, water, and starch together with enough
agitation and you get a sauce — glossy, slightly thickened, clinging to the
noodles. Toss oil and plain water and you get oily noodles in a puddle.

That transformation happens over about ninety seconds of tossing, and you can
see it. It is the single most useful pasta technique there is.

## Why cold-start the garlic

Conventional instruction is to heat the oil, then add the garlic. That works,
and it is faster, and the window before the garlic burns is roughly forty-five
seconds.

Starting cold — garlic and oil in the pan together, heat on afterward — widens
that window to several minutes. The garlic and the oil come up in temperature
together, so extraction happens gradually rather than the garlic searing on the
outside while the inside stays raw. You get more garlic flavor in the oil, more
evenly, with far less risk.

Slice rather than mince for the same reason. Minced garlic has enormous surface
area and burns almost instantly; slices are forgiving.

## The pasta water rule

Reserve more than you think, always, before draining. Every cook forgets this
once and then never again, because there is no substitute — plain water has no
starch and will not emulsify.

Undercook the pasta by two minutes on purpose. It finishes in the pan, absorbing
seasoned liquid instead of plain water, which seasons it from the inside. Pasta
finished in its sauce is a different dish from pasta with sauce spooned over it.

## Variations

- **With anchovy**: melt two fillets into the oil with the garlic. They dissolve
  completely and read as depth, not fish.
- **With breadcrumbs**: toasted coarse breadcrumbs on top. Crunch against soft is
  the cheapest upgrade available.
- **Cacio e pepe direction**: skip chile, toast cracked pepper in the oil, finish
  with pecorino off the heat. Same emulsion mechanics, harder to control.
- **With greens**: wilt broccoli rabe or spinach into the oil before the pasta.
- **With lemon**: zest at the end. Cuts the richness.

## What goes wrong

- **Burnt garlic.** Bitter, unfixable, start over. Cold start and low heat.
- **Oily puddle instead of sauce.** Not enough pasta water, not enough starch, or
  not enough tossing. Add more water and keep tossing over heat.
- **Bland pasta.** Under-salted water. The pasta itself is unseasoned and no
  amount of finishing salt fixes it.
- **Gluey and tight.** Too much starch, too little water. Loosen with more pasta
  water off the heat.
- **You rinsed the pasta.** You washed off the starch that was going to become
  your sauce. Never rinse pasta destined for a sauce.
- **Not enough oil.** A third of a cup for two people looks alarming and is
  correct. The oil is the sauce.
