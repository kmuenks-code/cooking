## Why this recipe

Because gluten is abstract until you have kneaded something.

You can read that glutenin provides elasticity and gliadin provides extensibility,
and it stays a fact about proteins. Then you knead a dough for eight minutes and
feel it change under your hands from a shaggy sticky mass into something smooth
and springy, and the fact becomes physical.

Bread teaches the same lesson, but the feedback loop is a day long and there is a
live organism involved that can fail independently. Fresh pasta is flour, egg,
salt, and your hands. Ninety minutes from start to eating, and every failure is
one of three variables.

## The three variables

Everything that goes wrong here is hydration, mixing, or resting.

**Hydration** — how much liquid per unit of flour. Egg is roughly 75 percent
water, so eggs are your hydration. Too little and the network cannot form; the
dough cracks and tears. Too much and it is slack and sticky and will not hold a
shape.

**Mixing** — the mechanical work that links the proteins. Under-knead and the
network is a collection of unconnected clumps, which is why under-kneaded pasta
disintegrates in the water. Over-kneading by hand is essentially impossible; you
will tire before the dough does.

**Resting** — time for hydration to complete and for the network to relax.
Skipping it is the most common failure and the most frustrating one, because the
dough fights you and it feels like you did something wrong when the answer is
simply to wait.

Feel all three and `ingr.flour-gluten` stops being a chapter and becomes a thing
you know.

## Ratios, and why they are approximate

The classical Italian ratio is **one egg per 100g of flour, per person.**

That is a starting point, not a specification, and this is the interesting part.
Flour absorbency varies with protein content, with the grind, and with the
humidity in your kitchen. Eggs vary in size by more than most people realize — a
"large" egg can be 45g or 55g out of the shell, which is a 20 percent swing in
your hydration.

So you weigh the flour, because that removes the variable you can control
completely, and then you adjust the dough by feel, because the other variable
cannot be removed. That combination — measure what you can, judge what you cannot
— is what `found.measuring` is actually arguing for.

By weight, this dough lands around **50 to 55 percent hydration**, which is stiff.
Bread runs 65 to 80. A pasta dough should feel noticeably firmer than any bread
dough you have handled.

## Flour choice

| Flour | Result |
| --- | --- |
| 00 (soft wheat, fine grind) | Silky, tender, classic for egg pasta. Traditional in the north of Italy |
| All-purpose | Perfectly good. Slightly more chew |
| Bread flour | Chewier and harder to roll. Not wrong, just different |
| Semolina (durum) | Very firm, needs water rather than egg. This is dry-pasta flour |
| 50/50 00 and semolina | A good compromise — holds shape better, still tender |

00 is a **grind classification**, not a protein specification, so read the bag.
There is 00 pizza flour and 00 pasta flour with meaningfully different protein
contents.

## Why semolina for dusting

Flour dusted on fresh pasta absorbs moisture from the dough surface and turns
gummy, which glues strands together and clouds the cooking water.

Semolina is coarser and much harder. It sits on the surface as discrete granules
and acts as tiny ball bearings, keeping the strands apart. It also mostly falls
off in the water rather than dissolving into it.

This is a small thing that makes a large difference, and it is the sort of detail
that separates pasta that unfurls cleanly from pasta that arrives as a brick.

## Fresh is not better

Worth saying plainly, because it is widely assumed otherwise.

Fresh egg pasta is **soft, silky, and tender**. It is right under butter, under
cream, under a long-cooked ragu, and as the wrapper for filled shapes.

Dry semolina pasta is **firm and springy with real bite**, and it holds al dente
for minutes rather than seconds. It is right for cacio e pepe, aglio e olio, most
tomato sauces, and anything where the pasta needs to hold up.

A good dry pasta beats mediocre fresh pasta at most things. Making it yourself
does not automatically improve the dish — it changes what the dish is. See
`prep.pasta`.

## Keeping it

- **Same day**: dusted with semolina, in loose nests on a tray, uncovered, a few
  hours.
- **Freezer**: nests frozen solid on a tray, then bagged. Cooks from frozen, add
  about a minute. This is the best option.
- **Dried**: only for shapes without egg, or thin shapes dried completely hard.
  Partially dried egg pasta can spoil.
- **Fridge**: the worst option. It absorbs moisture and goes sticky and grey
  within a day.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dough springs back, will not roll | Not rested | Cover, wait 10-30 min. Do not fight it |
| Tears while rolling | Too dry, or under-kneaded | Wet hands next time. Knead longer |
| Sticky and unmanageable | Too much egg for that flour | Dust and knead more flour in gradually |
| Falls apart in the water | Under-kneaded | Real gluten development. 8-10 minutes |
| Tough and rubbery | Too much flour, or rolled too thick | Weigh the flour. Roll thinner |
| Strands glued together | Dusted with flour, not semolina | Semolina, generously |
| Grey and gummy | Refrigerated overnight | Freeze instead |
| Mushy | Overcooked | 2-3 minutes maximum. Taste at 90 seconds |
| Bland | Water not salted | 10-20g per liter |
| Skin formed on the dough | Left uncovered | Cover every piece you are not rolling |
| Inconsistent every time | Measuring flour by volume | Weigh it. This is the recipe that proves it |
