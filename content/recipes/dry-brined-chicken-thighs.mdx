## Why this recipe

The salt lesson makes a strong claim: salting well in advance seasons meat all
the way through, helps it hold moisture, and dries the surface so it browns
better. Three benefits from one step.

It also links a video by Adam Ragusea arguing more or less the opposite.

So rather than asking you to take a side, this recipe runs both. Same chicken,
same oven, same pan, same total salt. The only variable is when it went on.

Taste both. Form your own view. That is a better outcome than believing either
of us.

## What dry brining does, in sequence

1. Salt draws moisture out of the surface by osmosis.
2. That moisture dissolves the salt into a concentrated brine sitting on the
   meat.
3. The brine is reabsorbed, carrying salt inward.
4. Salt partially denatures the muscle proteins so they hold more water when
   heated.
5. Meanwhile the refrigerator's dry air dehydrates the skin.

The awkward middle is real: between roughly two minutes and forty minutes, the
surface is wet from step 1 but step 3 has not happened yet. Salt then, and you
get the worst of both — a wet surface that browns badly and unseasoned meat.

So either salt right before cooking or well ahead. Do not land in between. Your
control group is deliberately at the ten-minute mark, which is close enough to
"right before" to be fine.

## Why uncovered, on a rack

Covering the chicken traps evaporating moisture against the skin and defeats the
drying half of the technique. The rack lets air circulate underneath so the
bottom dries too.

A refrigerator is a remarkably effective dehydrator — low humidity, constant
airflow. Twenty-four hours uncovered produces skin that browns and crisps in a
way a fresh thigh simply cannot, because a fresh thigh has to boil off its
surface water first, and while that is happening the surface is stuck at 212F.

## The baking powder question

A small amount of baking powder mixed with the salt raises the skin's surface pH.
The Maillard reaction runs faster in alkaline conditions, so the skin browns more
and crisps harder.

It works, and it is a genuine trick rather than folklore. It is also easy to
overdo — too much tastes soapy and metallic. A quarter teaspoon per pound,
mixed thoroughly with the salt, is the ceiling. Skip it on your first attempt so
you are testing one variable.

Baking *powder*, not baking soda. Soda is far more alkaline and will taste awful.

## Why 175F when 165F is safe

165F is the safety number for poultry. It is not the quality number for thighs.

Thigh is working muscle, full of connective tissue. That collagen needs to reach
roughly 160-180F and stay there to convert to gelatin, which is what makes a
thigh silky rather than rubbery. Pulling at 165F leaves you technically safe and
texturally worse.

Breast is the opposite — no meaningful connective tissue, so heat past doneness
only dries it out. Same bird, opposite strategies. This is the muscle-structure
idea in miniature.

## Variations

- **With vegetables underneath**: potatoes or carrots on the pan below the rack,
  catching the fat. One pan, whole dinner.
- **Spice-rubbed**: add paprika, cumin, coriander to the salt at brining time.
- **Buttermilk-brined**: swap dry brine for overnight in salted buttermilk. Acid
  plus salt plus dairy. Different mechanism, also excellent.
- **Under the broiler at the end**: two minutes for extra crisp, watched
  continuously.

## What goes wrong

- **Skin is flabby, not crisp.** Not dried enough, oven too cool, or thighs
  crowded so they steamed. Space them out.
- **Too salty.** You used table salt at kosher volumes. Up to double the sodium
  for the same measurement.
- **Dry and stringy.** Overcooked past 185F, or you used breasts and treated them
  like thighs.
- **Rubbery, chewy skin.** Undercooked. Thighs want more, not less.
- **Both groups taste identical.** Possible. Either the brine time was too short,
  or you seasoned the control more heavily by eye. Weigh the salt if you want a
  clean test.
