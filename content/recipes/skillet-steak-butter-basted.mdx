## Why this recipe

Because carryover cooking is invisible until you measure it, and a thick steak
seared hard is where it is largest.

Cook a 1.5-inch steak in a screaming pan, pull it at exactly 130F pleased with
yourself, rest it properly, and cut into grey medium-well meat. The pan did not
betray you. You just did not account for the fact that the steak's exterior was
several hundred degrees hotter than its center, and all that energy had to go
somewhere.

**Record both numbers.** Pull temperature and rested temperature. Once you have
seen the gap in your own kitchen, with your own pan, you will never cook a thick
protein by feel again.

## The pull temperatures

| Target doneness | Final temp | Pull at | Expect a rise of |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rare | 125F / 52C | 118-120F | 5-8F |
| Medium rare | 132F / 56C | 124-127F | 5-8F |
| Medium | 140F / 60C | 132-135F | 5-8F |
| Medium well | 150F / 65C | 143-145F | 5-7F |

These assume a thick cut seared hot. A thinner steak or a gentler cook carries
over less, because the temperature gradient between surface and center is
shallower. That relationship — steeper gradient means more carryover — is the
thing worth internalizing, not the specific numbers.

## Why the butter goes in last

Butter is roughly 18% water and 1-2% milk solids, and those solids scorch at
around 300F — well below searing temperature. Butter in the pan from the start
would be black before the crust formed.

So: sear in neutral oil, which tolerates the heat. Then drop the temperature and
add butter for the last ninety seconds, where it contributes flavor, browns its
milk solids to something nutty, and carries the garlic and thyme.

The foam is a useful signal. Loud foaming means water is still boiling off. When
it quiets and the solids start to color, you are in brown-butter territory —
which is delicious and about thirty seconds from burnt.

## Why basting works

Spooning hot fat over the top does two things at once.

It transfers heat to the upward-facing surface without flipping, so you get
gentler, more even cooking from above while the bottom keeps its contact with
the pan.

And it carries flavor. The garlic and thyme are sitting in that butter releasing
fat-soluble aromatic compounds, and every spoonful deposits them onto the crust.
This is the fat-as-solvent idea doing visible work.

Smashed garlic with skin on, deliberately — skin-on cloves are far more resistant
to burning at this temperature than peeled or chopped garlic.

## Resting, uncovered, on a rack

Uncovered and on a rack, not tented on a plate. Tenting traps steam against the
crust you just spent effort building and softens it. The temperature loss from
leaving it open for eight minutes is trivial; the crust loss from tenting is not.

Two things happen while it rests. Carryover finishes, which is the well-
established part. And moisture redistributes as the muscle fibers relax, which is
real but smaller and more contested than usually claimed. The carryover alone
justifies the rest.

## Variations

- **Reverse sear**: oven at 250F until the center hits 110F, then sear hard for
  90 seconds a side. Far more even edge-to-edge, and much less carryover because
  the gradient is shallow. Better technique, less dramatic lesson.
- **Compound butter finish**: skip basting, top the rested steak with herb butter.
- **Pan sauce after**: do not wash that pan. See the pan sauce recipe.
- **Cheaper cuts**: flat iron, bavette, or sirloin cap all take this treatment
  well at a fraction of the price.

## What goes wrong

- **Grey band under the crust.** Pan too hot for the thickness, or seared too
  long. Reverse sear fixes this properly.
- **Overcooked despite hitting your number.** You did not account for carryover.
  This is the lesson.
- **No crust.** Wet surface, pan not hot enough, or you moved it.
- **Kitchen full of smoke and a burnt taste.** Butter went in too early, or the
  oil was wrong. Sear in neutral oil.
- **Probe hit bone or fat.** Reads high, and you pull early. Push through and
  draw back, watching for the lowest number.
- **Sliced with the grain.** Chewy, regardless of how well you cooked it. Look at
  the fiber direction and cut across it.
