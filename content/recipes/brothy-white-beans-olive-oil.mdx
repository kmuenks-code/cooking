## Why this recipe

Because the fat lesson is hard to feel in a dish that already has butter in the
pan, cheese on top, and oil in the dressing. Here, everything before the last
step is water, beans, and aromatics. Plain on purpose.

Then a third of a cup of good olive oil goes in and the dish changes completely.
That gap is the lesson.

**Taste it before and after.** Not metaphorically — take a spoonful of the broth
before you pour the oil, then another after. The plain version is savory and
thin and finishes almost immediately. The oiled version is round, keeps
delivering flavor after you swallow, and coats your mouth in a way that reads as
satisfying. Nothing was added but fat.

## Why salt the beans from the start

You have probably been told salt toughens beans and should go in at the end.

This is largely wrong, and it has been tested repeatedly. Salted beans cook to
tender in about the same time, season all the way through rather than just on
the surface, and — counterintuitively — hold their skins *better*, because
sodium ions displace calcium and magnesium in the skin's pectin and make it more
permeable rather than less.

The real culprits behind beans that never soften are **acid** and **age**. Adding
tomatoes, vinegar, or wine early genuinely does keep beans firm, because acid
strengthens pectin. And beans more than a year or two old may simply never get
there. If your beans are refusing to cook, check those two things before blaming
the salt.

## Why a bare simmer

A rolling boil agitates the beans against each other and against the pot,
breaking skins and clouding the broth. A bare simmer cooks them just as fast —
the water is essentially the same temperature — with far less mechanical damage.

This is the same principle as poaching, and it is the difference between intact
creamy beans in clear broth and a pot of grey mush.

## The canned shortcut

Three cans, drained and rinsed, simmered 20 minutes with the aromatics and
enough water to cover generously. Not as good — the broth is thinner because you
did not get the starch that leaches out over a long cook — but it takes 25
minutes and the olive oil lesson lands just as clearly.

## Variations

- **With greens**: stir in kale, chard, or escarole for the last ten minutes.
- **With a parmesan rind**: in from the start. Umami and body, for free.
- **On toast**: pile beans and broth over grilled bread rubbed with garlic.
- **Brothier or thicker**: mash a ladleful of beans against the side of the pot
  and stir back in. Instant body, no starch added.

## What goes wrong

- **Beans still chalky after two hours.** Old beans, or acid went in early. Not
  the salt.
- **Mush.** Boiled too hard, or overcooked. Bare simmer, and start tasting early.
- **The broth is thin and boring.** Not enough salt, or too much water. Simmer
  uncovered to concentrate.
- **You used cheap oil.** This recipe is a delivery mechanism for good olive oil.
  With supermarket generic it is a decent bowl of beans and the lesson does not
  land.
- **You added the oil during cooking.** Its flavor compounds are volatile and an
  hour of simmering destroys them. Off the heat, at the end, in the bowl.
