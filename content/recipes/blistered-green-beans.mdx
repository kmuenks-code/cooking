## Why this recipe

Because "don't crowd the pan" is advice everyone has heard and almost nobody has
actually seen demonstrated. Here you cook the same beans, in the same pan, at the
same temperature, twice — and you get to watch what happens when the pan has
enough stored energy and when it does not.

Vegetables are a better vehicle for this than meat, because there is no doneness
target to worry about and the visual difference is stark. Blistered beans are
brown and blackened in patches. Steamed beans are uniformly dull olive green.
You cannot mistake one for the other.

## What crowding actually does

Two separate mechanisms, running at once:

**Thermal.** Your pan is holding a finite amount of energy. Every bean you add is
cold mass that draws some of it out. Add too many and the surface temperature
falls below the ~300F needed for browning, and the pan cannot recover because
the beans keep pulling heat.

**Atmospheric.** Beans are mostly water, and heat drives that water out as steam.
In a spaced-out pan the steam escapes. In a crowded pan it forms a humid layer
around the beans and holds their surfaces at 212F — below browning temperature,
by definition.

So a crowded pan fails twice, for two different reasons, and neither is fixable
by waiting longer. You just get steamed beans that eventually dry out.

**The tell is sound.** A proper sear is loud and stays loud. If the noise drops
to a wet murmur within thirty seconds of the food landing, you are steaming.

## The counterexample worth running once

Cook the whole pound at once, deliberately, and look at the result. It is the
single fastest way to make the crowding rule permanent — you will never again
wonder whether it is worth the extra batch.

## Why not olive oil

You are running this pan hot enough that extra virgin olive oil is at or past
its useful range. Its distinctive flavor compounds are volatile and degrade well
before the smoke point, so you would be paying for flavor you then destroy.

Neutral oil for the heat. If you want olive oil flavor, add a little raw at the
end with the lemon.

## Variations

- **Blistered shishitos or padrón peppers**: same technique, three minutes total.
- **With anchovy**: melt one into the pan with the garlic. Umami, and nobody will
  identify it.
- **Sichuan-style dry-fried**: add ground pork and preserved vegetable with the
  garlic.
- **Almonds and brown butter**: swap the neutral oil finish for butter cooked to
  nutty, plus toasted almonds.
- **Broccolini, asparagus, cabbage wedges**: all work. Denser vegetables want a
  splash of water and a lid for a minute after blistering.

## What goes wrong

- **Uniformly green, no color, liquid in the pan.** Crowded, or wet, or the pan
  was not hot enough. Usually all three.
- **Garlic burnt and bitter.** It went in too early or too hot. Garlic goes in at
  the end, at reduced heat, for under a minute.
- **Beans blackened but squeaky raw inside.** Pan too hot for their thickness.
  Lower the heat slightly, or add a splash of water and cover for a minute.
- **Smoking violently.** Wrong oil, or the pan sat empty on high too long.
- **Limp and greasy.** Too much oil. Two tablespoons coats; more shallow-fries.
