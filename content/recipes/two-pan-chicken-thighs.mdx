## Why this recipe

The pan lesson makes an abstract claim — that thermal mass determines whether a
pan can hold its temperature when cold food lands on it, and that this matters
more than material or price.

Reading that is unconvincing. Hearing one pan keep sizzling while the other goes
quiet, thirty seconds apart, is not.

This is the cheapest possible controlled experiment: one variable, two
simultaneous outcomes, and you eat the evidence.

## What you should observe

**Preheat time.** The thin pan reaches searing temperature faster — less mass to
heat. This is the upside of low thermal mass, and it is the only round the thin
pan wins.

**The moment the chicken lands.** The heavy pan barely notices. The thin pan's
temperature drops sharply, because the chicken is drawing more energy than the
pan can supply. You hear this before you see it.

**The crust.** The heavy pan should give a more even, deeper brown. The thin pan
often produces patchy color, or a pale grey surface if the drop was severe
enough.

**Liquid in the pan.** If a pan's surface fell below 212F, the chicken's released
moisture pools instead of evaporating, and the chicken is now poaching in it.
There will usually be noticeably more liquid in the thin pan.

**Fond.** The heavy pan should leave more browned material stuck to the bottom —
more browning happened. That fond is worth something, which is what the pan sauce
recipe uses.

## Why the counterintuitive result happens

The thin pan got hotter, faster. It still browned worse.

Because pan temperature before the food lands is not the variable that matters.
What matters is the temperature *while the food is cooking*, and that depends on
how much energy the pan had banked and how fast the burner can resupply it.

A thin pan is a small reservoir. A heavy pan is a large one. Cold food is a
withdrawal. This is the whole reason "don't crowd the pan" is a rule, and it is
why it matters far more in a light pan than a heavy one.

## Complicating it honestly

Some caveats, because this is an experiment and experiments deserve honesty:

- **Burner dials are not calibrated between burners.** Even on the same stove,
  two burners at "medium-high" may differ. Swap the pans between burners and run
  it again if you want to rule this out.
- **Pan diameter changes results.** A wider pan means more spacing per thigh,
  which independently helps. Match diameters as closely as you can.
- **Material and mass are confounded here.** Cast iron is both heavier and a
  different material. Separating those would need a thick and thin pan of the
  same material — worth doing if you own them.

None of this undermines the demonstration. It just means you have shown that
*this* pan beat *that* pan, and the mechanism is the most likely explanation
rather than the proven one.

## Variations

- **Same pan, crowded vs. spaced**: hold the pan constant and vary the amount of
  food instead. Isolates the crowding variable cleanly.
- **Nonstick vs. stainless**: the fond difference becomes stark. Nonstick gives
  you essentially none.
- **Wet vs. dry surface**: pat one batch dry and leave one damp, same pan. This
  is the searing lesson's practice prompt.

## What goes wrong

- **You adjusted a burner mid-cook.** Understandable, and it ruins the
  comparison. Let the bad pan be bad.
- **Both pans performed identically.** They were probably too similar. You need a
  real contrast in weight.
- **The thin-pan chicken is overcooked and still pale.** That is the expected
  failure and it is the finding, not a mistake.
- **You cooked them in sequence instead of simultaneously.** Kitchen conditions
  drift. Simultaneous is the point.
