## Why this recipe

Because it is the first dish where everything you have learned so far is load-
bearing at the same time.

The pan choice determines whether you get fond. Heat control determines whether
the skin renders or burns. The dry surface determines whether it browns. The
thermometer determines whether the chicken is right. Salt timing determines
whether it is seasoned or merely salty. And the sauce is built entirely from
what the searing left behind.

Get one of those wrong and you notice. That is the point of putting it at the end
of the path.

## The sequence, memorized as a shape

Forget this recipe. Remember this:

```
sear  →  rest  →  pour off fat  →  aromatics
      →  deglaze with acid  →  reduce with stock
      →  off heat, mount with cold butter
      →  resting juices, acid, herbs, salt last
```

That template works with pork and cider, steak and red wine, fish and vermouth,
chicken and sherry. Once you have it, you can build a pan sauce out of whatever
is in the kitchen, which is exactly the kind of thinking the last module of this
curriculum is about.

## Why pour off the fat

The most-skipped step and the most common cause of failure.

At the end you are making an emulsion — butterfat suspended as droplets in a
water-based reduction. Emulsions have a limit to how much fat they can hold
before the droplets find each other and merge back into a layer.

Chicken thighs render a lot of fat. Leave it all in and you have exceeded that
limit before you start. The sauce will separate into oil floating on thin brown
liquid, no matter how carefully you swirl the butter.

One tablespoon. Pour the rest into a jar — it is schmaltz, and it is excellent
for roasting potatoes.

## Why cold butter, off the heat

Butter is an emulsion already: butterfat, water, and a small amount of milk
proteins and phospholipids that act as emulsifiers.

Add it to a hot pan and it melts instantly, the structure collapses, and the fat
separates out as a layer. Add it cold, off the heat, swirling — and it melts
gradually, giving those emulsifiers time to position themselves around the fat
droplets as they form.

Same physics as hollandaise, much lower stakes. If it breaks, pull it off the
heat and whisk in a splash of cold stock or water to bring it back.

## Why salt goes last

Reduction concentrates everything, salt included. A sauce seasoned correctly when
you added the stock will be aggressively salty once it has reduced by half.

Stock is the variable that catches people — commercial stock is often already
well salted, and reducing it by half roughly doubles that. Taste after reducing,
after mounting, and only then decide.

## Why the final acid matters

You have reduced stock, mounted butter, and rendered chicken fat into a sauce
that is rich by construction. Without acid it reads heavy and tiring, and the
usual instinct is to add more salt, which does not help.

Two teaspoons of lemon or vinegar at the end, off the heat, is what makes it
taste finished rather than merely rich. It is the single most common omission in
home pan sauces.

## Variations

- **Pork and cider**: cider to deglaze, cider vinegar at the end, mustard whisked
  in with the butter, thyme.
- **Steak and red wine**: red wine, beef stock, more shallot, reduce harder.
- **Chicken and sherry with mushrooms**: mushrooms sauteed with the shallot,
  sherry to deglaze.
- **Fish and vermouth**: vermouth, a splash of cream instead of some of the
  butter, dill.
- **Miso butter**: whisk a teaspoon of miso into the stock. Umami without
  anything reading as Japanese.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greasy, oil floating on top | Too much fat left, or butter added too hot | Pour off more next time; rescue with cold stock whisked in off heat |
| Thin and watery | Not reduced enough, or thin stock | Keep reducing. Gelatin-poor stock will never go silky |
| Bitter | Fond burnt, or aromatics scorched | Not fixable. Watch the pan after the sear |
| Too salty | Seasoned before reducing, or salty stock | Add unsalted liquid and re-reduce |
| Flat despite salt | Missing the final acid | Lemon or vinegar, off the heat |
| Skin went soft | Sauce poured over the chicken, or it was covered while resting | Spoon around, rest uncovered |
| No fond at all | Nonstick pan, or the chicken was wet and steamed | Stainless or carbon steel, dry surface |
