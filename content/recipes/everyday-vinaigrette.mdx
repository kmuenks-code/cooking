## Why this recipe first

Because it is the smallest possible complete dish. Four ingredients, no heat, no
timing, nothing to overcook. Every variable you can change is a flavor decision,
which makes it the clearest place to feel what acid actually does.

It is also a ratio in disguise, and ratios are where the whole curriculum is
eventually heading. **Three parts oil to one part acid** is the classical
starting point. Not a rule — a place to start tasting from.

## What is actually happening

Oil and vinegar do not mix. Left alone they separate within seconds, because
oil molecules and water molecules have no interest in each other.

The mustard is what changes that. It contains compounds that are attracted to
both, so they position themselves at the boundary between oil droplet and
vinegar and stop the droplets from finding each other and merging. Whisking
provides the force that breaks the oil into droplets in the first place; the
mustard is what keeps them broken.

That is an emulsion, and it is the same mechanism as mayonnaise and hollandaise
— just far more forgiving, because there is no heat and no egg to worry about.

If you leave it sitting, it will eventually separate anyway. Whisk it again. A
vinaigrette held together by mustard alone is a temporary emulsion.

## The 3:1 ratio is a starting point, not a rule

Adjust it deliberately and notice what happens:

- **More oil (4:1, 5:1)** — rounder, softer, better on bitter or assertive
  greens that need something to push against.
- **More acid (2:1)** — sharper, brighter, better on rich things. Excellent on
  a bean salad or over something fried.
- **Different acid entirely** — the single change that most transforms the
  dressing. Lemon is bright and floral. Sherry vinegar is nutty and deep. Rice
  vinegar is mild and low-acid, so you will need less oil to balance it.

The point of the practice exercise is to make one of these changes on purpose
and taste the difference, rather than following a number.

## Variations

- **Honey mustard**: add 1/2 tsp honey. Sweetness balances acid, which is worth
  feeling directly.
- **Herby**: whisk in chopped parsley, chives, or tarragon at the end.
- **Creamy**: replace 1 tbsp of the oil with yogurt or buttermilk. Acid *and*
  fat *and* body, in one ingredient.
- **Garlic**: grate a small clove into the vinegar with the shallot. It will get
  stronger over a day, not weaker.

## What goes wrong

- **It separates immediately.** Not enough mustard, or the oil went in too fast.
  Add the oil more slowly and keep whisking.
- **It tastes harsh.** Too much acid for the oil, or an aggressive vinegar. More
  oil, or a pinch of sugar.
- **It tastes flat and greasy.** Not enough acid or not enough salt. Salt first
  — it dissolves in the vinegar, not in the oil.
- **The salad is soggy.** Too much dressing, or dressed too early. Both are
  fixable next time; neither is fixable now.
- **You tasted it off a spoon and made it milder.** Always taste it on the food.
  A dressing that tastes right alone will be bland once distributed.
