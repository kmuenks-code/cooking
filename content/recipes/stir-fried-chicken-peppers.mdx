## Why this recipe

Because the mise en place lesson's claim — that some cooking steps cannot be
paused — is easy to nod along to and hard to feel until you are standing at a
smoking wok with twenty seconds before the garlic burns and no minced ginger.

A stir-fry has almost no pausable steps. Total cooking time is under six minutes.
The garlic window is twenty seconds. Chopping while this runs is not a
suboptimal choice; it is not a choice at all.

The prep-to-cook ratio here is roughly 15 minutes to 4. That looks wrong and it
is correct.

## Grouping, not just chopping

The specific instruction worth attention: **group by the moment ingredients go
in, not by what they are.**

Garlic and ginger hit the pan together, so they go in one bowl. Peppers and onion
go in together, so they share a bowl. That is four containers for this whole
dish, not eleven — and it eliminates most of what makes mise en place feel like
fussy overhead.

Then line them up left to right in order. You stop having to think about what is
next; you just move right. When the pan demands your full attention, this is what
lets you give it.

## Why the chicken comes out and goes back in

Two reasons, both thermal.

Chicken and vegetables cook at different rates. Leaving both in means one is
wrong.

More importantly, everything you add draws energy out of the pan. Chicken plus
peppers plus onion at once, in a home-kitchen skillet, drops the surface
temperature below browning immediately. You end up with grey chicken and limp
vegetables stewing in released liquid — the crowding failure from the green beans
recipe, with more mass.

Cooking in stages is how you work around a home burner that puts out a fraction
of a restaurant wok range's heat.

## Velveting

The cornstarch coating is the one genuinely non-obvious technique here.

Cornstarch and liquid form a thin gel layer on the chicken's surface. That layer
takes the brunt of the pan's heat, so the meat underneath cooks more gently and
stays tender rather than seizing and drying. It also helps the sauce cling at the
end.

It is why restaurant stir-fried chicken has a texture home versions usually
don't, and it costs one tablespoon and zero extra time.

## Working with a home stove

Restaurant woks run at heat levels a domestic burner cannot approach, and most
home stir-fry disappointment traces to that gap. Practical compensations:

- **Use your heaviest pan**, not a thin wok. Thermal mass substitutes for burner
  output. A flat-bottomed carbon steel skillet often beats a round wok on an
  electric hob.
- **Preheat far longer than feels necessary.** Until it genuinely smokes.
- **Cook in more, smaller batches.** Two batches of chicken beats one.
- **Dry everything.** Wet vegetables dump water and stall the pan.

## Variations

- **Any protein**: beef, pork, shrimp, tofu. Velvet them all the same way.
- **Any vegetable that stays crisp**: broccoli, snap peas, green beans, bok choy.
- **Spicy**: dried chiles into the oil before the chicken, or chile crisp at the
  end.
- **Black bean**: swap oyster sauce for fermented black beans mashed with the
  garlic.

## What goes wrong

- **Everything steamed and grey.** Crowded, wet, or the pan was never hot enough.
  Usually all three.
- **Burnt garlic.** It went in too early or sat too long. Twenty seconds, in a
  cleared space, near the end.
- **Chicken tough and dry.** Skipped the velveting, or cooked it too long. It
  finishes when it goes back in with the sauce.
- **Watery sauce pooling in the bottom.** Vegetables released moisture into a pan
  that was too cool. Higher heat, smaller batches.
- **You started cooking before prep was done.** The lesson, delivered the hard
  way.
- **Sesame oil added early.** It is a finishing oil. Heat destroys it.
