## Why this recipe

Because umami is hard to notice in a dish that already has a lot going on.

Add an anchovy to a tomato sauce and the sauce gets better, but the sauce was
already good, and it is genuinely difficult to attribute the improvement. Add
parmesan to a rich pasta and of course it improves — parmesan improves most
things.

Lentils simmered in water are close to a blank background. Salt them properly and
they still taste like lentils and water. That flatness is the instrument: it means
the umami additions are audible, and you can hear what each one does.

Cook this once as written. Then, if you want the lesson to land completely, cook a
small pot of lentils with the same aromatic base and **no** anchovy, rind, or
tomato paste, and taste them side by side. The difference is not subtle and it is
not explained by the calories or the salt.

## The multiplication

Three umami sources, and they are deliberately not from the same family. From
`flavor.umami`:

| Source | Family | Compound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tomato paste | Glutamate | Free glutamic acid |
| Parmesan rind | Both | Glutamate from ageing, plus nucleotides |
| Anchovy | Nucleotide | Inosinate |

Glutamates and nucleotides do not add. They **multiply** — the nucleotide raises
the receptor's sensitivity to glutamate, so a small amount of one alongside the
other produces an effect several times what either delivers alone.

This is why three anchovies and a scrap of rind transform three cups of water,
and why using three times as much tomato paste instead would not.

It is also the explanation for a long list of combinations people arrived at
empirically: dashi from kombu and bonito, parmesan on tomato sauce, bacon with
tomato, mushrooms with beef. Nobody knew the mechanism. Everybody could taste the
result.

## Why the anchovies disappear

Anchovies packed in oil or salt have been cured for months, and that cure has
broken their proteins down substantially. They are soft, and mashed into warm fat
they dissolve completely within thirty seconds.

That dissolution is not cosmetic. A dissolved anchovy distributes through the
entire pot as inosinate and salt, and reads as **depth**. An anchovy left in
pieces reads as **anchovy**, and someone at the table will find it and have
opinions.

Three fillets in four servings is well under the threshold where anyone identifies
fish. This is why a lot of restaurant vegetable dishes taste better than the same
dish at home, and why so few of them mention it on the menu.

Vegetarian substitution: a tablespoon of white miso whisked in off the heat at the
end, plus two dried shiitake dropped in with the water. That gives you glutamate
from the miso and guanylate from the mushrooms — a different pairing, same
multiplication.

## Why salt comes late

Two reasons, and they point the same way.

**Glutamate makes food taste saltier.** Salt something before the umami sources
have released into the liquid and you will calibrate against a flatter dish than
the one you end up with, and overshoot.

**Reduction concentrates.** The pot loses water over thirty-five minutes, and the
rind contributes its own salt, and the anchovies contribute a surprising amount.
A pot salted at the start is often aggressively salty at the end.

The old claim that salt prevents beans and lentils from softening does not survive
testing — if anything salted legumes cook slightly faster, because sodium ions
displace calcium and magnesium in the pectin of the skins and weaken them. Salting
late here is about seasoning control, not about texture. See `ingr.legumes`.

## Variations

- **With sausage**: brown a crumbled Italian sausage first, build the base in its
  fat. The pork adds more inosinate.
- **With pancetta or bacon**: same, and you can skip the anchovy.
- **Fully vegetarian**: miso plus dried shiitake, as above.
- **With greens**: a bunch of chopped kale or chard in for the last ten minutes.
- **Soupier**: another cup of water, and serve it as a soup with bread.
- **Cold, as a salad**: cook drier, cool, and dress heavily with vinaigrette and
  raw shallot.
- **Under fish**: this is the classic pairing. A piece of roast cod or salmon on
  top, and the lentils become the sauce.

## What goes wrong

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tastes of fish | Anchovies not fully dissolved | Mash into bare hot fat until gone |
| Still flat | One umami source instead of two families | Rind plus anchovy, not more of either |
| Too salty | Salted at the start | Rind and anchovy bring salt. Season at the end |
| Lentils turned to mush | Boiled, or red lentils used | Bare simmer, brown or green lentils |
| Lentils still hard after an hour | Old lentils | Not fixable in the pot. Keep going and buy fresher |
| Skins came off | Boiled too hard | Gentle simmer from the start |
| Tastes heavy | No acid | Vinegar off the heat |
| Watery | Too much liquid, or lid on the whole time | Partly covered, and reduce at the end |
| Raw onion note | Base under-sweated | 15 minutes. It does not cook out later |
| Bitter | Burnt garlic | Garlic after the onion, 45 seconds |
