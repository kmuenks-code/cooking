## Why "cook for 20 minutes" cannot work

A recipe's cooking time is a guess built on assumptions about your oven's actual
temperature, the starting temperature of your food, its thickness, its shape, its
fat content, the pan it sits in, and how often you open the door.

Thickness alone breaks it. Two chicken breasts of the same weight can differ by
half an inch in thickness, and cooking time scales with something closer to the
square of thickness than with weight. One is done; the other is raw in the
middle. Both weighed the same and went in at the same time.

Meanwhile, **temperature is the thing you actually care about.** Doneness is not
a duration — it is a set of physical changes in proteins that happen at specific
temperatures. Measuring the variable directly, instead of inferring it from a
proxy, is not a shortcut. It is the correct move.

An instant-read thermometer costs about twenty dollars and is the single highest
value purchase in this entire curriculum.

## What is actually happening at temperature

Doneness is protein denaturation. Proteins are folded chains; heat unfolds them
and they bond to each other, which is what turns translucent raw meat into opaque
firm cooked meat. Different proteins unfold at different temperatures, which is
why doneness is a sequence of events rather than a single switch.

The landmarks worth knowing:

| Around | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 120F / 49C | Myosin denatures. Meat turns from translucent to opaque, firms slightly. Rare. |
| 140F / 60C | Muscle fibers contract sharply and squeeze out moisture. This is where juiciness starts leaving. |
| 150-155F / 65-68C | Most remaining proteins set. Meat is noticeably firmer and drier. |
| 160F+ / 71C+ | Actin denatures fully. Meat is firm and dry. Unavoidably. |
| 160-180F / 71-82C, held over time | Collagen converts to gelatin. This needs *hours*, not minutes. |

That last row is the one that explains braising, and it explains a thing that
otherwise looks like a contradiction. Why is a slow-cooked pork shoulder at 200F
tender when a pork chop at 160F is dry?

Because they are different problems. The chop is tender to begin with, and heat
only makes it worse — you want to stop early. The shoulder is full of tough
connective tissue, and the only way to convert that collagen to gelatin is a long
hold at high temperature. You destroy the muscle fibers completely and get
tenderness back from the gelatin instead. Two opposite strategies, and knowing
which one you are in decides everything.

## Using a thermometer

The instrument is easy. The probe placement is where people go wrong.

- **Thickest part, away from bone.** Bone conducts heat differently and will read
  higher than the surrounding meat, so a probe touching bone tells you the bone
  is done.
- **Avoid large fat pockets and gaps.** Fat and air both read differently from
  muscle.
- **Push the probe through and pull it back slowly, watching the number.** You are
  hunting for the *lowest* reading, which is the thermal center. That is the
  number that matters. A single insertion at a guessed depth is the most common
  error.
- **Go in from the side on thin items.** For a steak or a fillet, entering from
  the edge horizontally puts the tip in the middle. Entering from the top on a
  one-inch steak means you are measuring nothing useful.
- **Check more than one piece.** They are not identical.

For a roast that will be in the oven for hours, a leave-in probe thermometer with
an external display is worth having — you stop opening the oven, which is itself
a source of error.

## Carryover cooking

This is the part that turns a correctly measured cook into an overcooked one.

When you take food off the heat, the outside is much hotter than the inside. That
energy does not vanish. It keeps flowing inward, and the internal temperature
keeps climbing for several minutes after the food has left the pan or oven.

**How much it climbs depends on two things:**

- **Mass.** A large roast holds far more energy than a thin cutlet, and drives a
  larger rise. A big prime rib can climb 10 to 15F. A thin fish fillet might
  climb 2F.
- **The steepness of the temperature gradient.** Food cooked in a very hot
  environment has a much hotter exterior relative to its center, so it has more
  stored energy to push inward. This is why a steak seared hard in a screaming
  pan carries over more than the same steak cooked slowly at 250F.

Practical rules:

| Item | Pull it at | Expect a rise of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thin cutlet, fish fillet | 2-3F below target | 2-3F |
| Steak or chop, seared hot | 5-10F below target | 5-10F |
| Whole chicken | 5-10F below target | 5-10F |
| Large roast | 10-15F below target | 10-15F |
| Anything cooked low and slow | 0-3F below target | Minimal, the gradient is shallow |

If you have ever hit exactly 130F on a steak, been pleased, and then cut into
grey medium-well meat ten minutes later, this is what happened.

## Resting, and what it is actually for

Two things happen while meat rests, and they are commonly conflated.

**Carryover finishes.** The temperature equalizes and the center reaches its final
temperature. This is the part above.

**Moisture redistributes.** During cooking, muscle fibers contract and force
liquid toward the center. Cutting immediately releases that concentrated liquid
onto the board. Resting lets it move back out through the meat and lets the fibers
relax enough to hold onto it.

The second effect is real but smaller and more contested than the confident
version you usually hear. There is decent evidence that its magnitude has been
overstated, and Kenji among others has published measurements pushing back on the
standard claims. What is not contested is the carryover effect, which by itself
justifies resting.

Rough guidance: five minutes for a steak or chop, ten to twenty for a chicken,
twenty to thirty for a large roast. Tent loosely with foil if you are worried
about heat loss, but do not wrap tightly — trapped steam will soften a crust you
just spent effort creating.

## Where the safety chart and the cooking chart disagree

The safety numbers from the previous lesson and the texture numbers here do not
always agree, and it is worth being honest about it.

Chicken breast is safe at 165F and unpleasant there. Many experienced cooks
target 150-155F, because pathogen destruction is a function of *time at
temperature* rather than a single instantaneous threshold — chicken held at 150F
for several minutes is as safe as chicken that briefly touched 165F.

That is a legitimate, informed choice. It is not the same as guessing. Make it
knowingly, understand that it requires actually measuring, and do not make it
when cooking for anyone pregnant, elderly, very young, or immunocompromised.

Pork is a cleaner story: the USDA revised its guidance to 145F plus a rest years
ago, and the well-done grey pork chop most of us grew up with is a holdover from
a trichinosis risk that is functionally gone in commercial pork.

## The failure modes

- **Measuring in the wrong place.** Bone, fat, or not deep enough. The most
  common cause of a wrong reading.
- **Not accounting for carryover.** Pulling at target instead of below it.
- **Measuring one piece and assuming the rest match.** They do not.
- **Cutting into it to check.** You lose juice and you still cannot see
  temperature. This is what the thermometer replaced.
- **Resting a braise.** Not needed. There is no gradient to equalize and nothing
  to redistribute; it has been sitting in liquid at a uniform temperature for
  hours.
- **Applying tender-cut logic to tough cuts.** Pulling a brisket at 145F gives you
  a piece of meat you cannot chew. Different problem, different rules.
