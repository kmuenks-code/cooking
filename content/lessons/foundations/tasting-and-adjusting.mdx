## The difference this makes

Two people cook the same recipe. One follows it exactly and serves what comes
out. The other tastes at four points, adds salt twice, adds a squeeze of lemon
at the end, and pulls it off the heat two minutes early because it looked right.

The second dish is better, every time, and it is not because that cook has a
better palate. It is because they were asking questions during the cook, while
answers were still cheap.

This is the highest-leverage habit in the curriculum and it requires no
equipment, no ingredient, and no technique. It is placed at the very start, with
no prerequisites, because everything in Flavor Fundamentals is unusable without
it. Learning what acid does is pointless if you never taste a dish and notice
that it needs some.

## Tasting is not liking

Here is the reframe that makes this a skill rather than an opinion.

Most people taste food and produce a verdict: good, fine, not great. A verdict
is nearly useless, because it does not tell you what to do next.

A diagnosis does. The question is not *is this good* but **what is this
missing** — and there is a short list of possible answers. For savory cooking,
nearly everything comes down to:

| If it tastes... | It is probably missing... | Try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flat, dull, like nothing | **Salt** | Salt, in small increments |
| Heavy, rich, cloying, tiring | **Acid** | Lemon, vinegar, a pickled thing |
| Thin, hollow, technically fine but unsatisfying | **Umami** | Anchovy, soy, tomato paste, parmesan, mushroom, fish sauce |
| Harsh, sharp, aggressive | **Fat** or **sweetness** | Butter, oil, cream, or a pinch of sugar |
| Muddy, one-note, tiring by the third bite | **Contrast** | Fresh herbs, crunch, something raw or bright on top |
| Correct but boring | **Texture** | Something crisp, or a temperature difference |

That table is the entire Troubleshooting module in miniature, and you can start
using it tonight. It will be wrong sometimes. It is still enormously better than
"needs something."

The most common single error, by a wide margin, is reaching for salt when the
dish needs acid. Rich food that keeps asking for more salt and never seems to
get there is almost always asking for acid instead. If you take one thing from
this lesson, take that.

## When to taste

Tasting constantly is not the goal. Tasting at the moments where the information
is actionable is.

**Taste the components before they go in.** Your stock, your tomatoes, your
cheese, your olive oil. Ingredients vary enormously — two cans of tomatoes from
different brands can differ in acidity and sweetness more than most recipe
adjustments would. If you do not know what went in, you cannot reason about what
came out.

**Taste after the base is built.** Once the aromatics are cooked and the liquid
is in, you have the foundation of the dish's flavor. This is the cheapest moment
to correct, because everything after this amplifies whatever is already there.

**Taste during reduction, repeatedly.** This is the one that catches people.
Reduction concentrates *everything*, salt included. A sauce seasoned correctly
at the start will be oversalted by the time it has reduced by half. Season
reductions at the end, and taste on the way.

**Taste right before serving.** The last call, and the moment for acid and fresh
herbs, both of which fade with heat and time.

### When tasting misleads you

Three cases worth knowing, because they cause confident wrong decisions:

- **Very hot food tastes less salty and less sweet than it will at eating
  temperature.** If you season a soup to taste while it is scalding, it will be
  oversalted in the bowl. Let the spoonful cool for a few seconds.
- **Raw components taste nothing like their cooked selves.** Raw onion is sharp,
  cooked onion is sweet. Raw flour in a roux tastes like paste. Do not correct
  for a flavor that the cooking process is going to remove.
- **Palate fatigue is real.** After five or six tastes of the same thing you stop
  registering it accurately, and salt is the first thing to go numb. If you have
  been tasting a sauce repeatedly and it keeps seeming underseasoned, stop.
  Drink some water, wait a minute, or ask someone else.

## How to adjust

**Small increments, then wait, then re-taste.** Salt takes a moment to dissolve
and distribute — adding it and tasting immediately tells you about the spoon,
not the pot. Give it fifteen or twenty seconds.

**One variable at a time.** If you add salt and lemon and butter simultaneously
and it gets better, you have learned nothing you can reuse. Adding one thing and
noticing the change is how the diagnostic table above gets built into your head
rather than staying on a page.

**Know which direction is reversible.** You can always add more salt. You can
almost never remove it. The same is true of acid, heat, and most strong
flavorings. This asymmetry should make you systematically under-add and approach
from below, which feels slow and is correct.

**Undersalt the pot when the components will be salted later.** If the cheese,
the olives, the soy, or the cured meat is still to come, they bring salt with
them.

## Building the habit

The obstacle is not skill, it is remembering to do it. A few things that help:

- **Keep a spoon in your hand or in the pot.** Friction is why people do not
  taste. Removing the friction is most of the fix.
- **Salt from a bowl, not a shaker.** Pinching salt with your fingers gives you
  a physical sense of quantity that shaking never will, and it makes small
  increments natural.
- **Say the diagnosis out loud, even if it is wrong.** "This is flat." "This is
  too rich." Naming forces specificity in a way that a vague impression does not,
  and being wrong out loud is how the vocabulary gets calibrated.
- **Taste other people's food this way too,** including restaurant food. Ask what
  is making it work. This is free practice and it is where reverse-engineering
  later in the curriculum starts.

## The failure modes

- **Not tasting until the end.** By then most corrections are expensive or
  impossible.
- **Tasting without a question.** Produces a verdict, not an action.
- **Correcting in one big move.** Overshooting is how a slightly flat dish
  becomes an inedibly salty one.
- **Trusting the recipe's quantity over your own mouth.** The recipe writer used
  different salt, different tomatoes, a different palate, and a different pan.
  Their number is a starting point.
- **Tasting only the sauce.** Taste the thing you are going to eat — the sauce
  *with* the pasta, the dressing *on* the greens. A dressing that tastes
  aggressive alone is often exactly right once it is distributed.
