## The flour problem

Take a measuring cup and scoop it through a bag of flour, then level it off.
Weigh it. Now spoon flour gently into the same cup and level it. Weigh that.

The first will land somewhere near 150 to 170 grams. The second, around 120 to
125. Same cup, same flour, same person — and a difference of thirty to forty
percent.

Flour is a compressible powder. How much of a given volume is flour and how much
is air depends entirely on how it got in there. Scooping packs it. Spooning does
not. Sifting first makes it lighter again. A humid kitchen makes it heavier.

Forty percent is not a rounding error. In a cake it is the difference between
tender and dry. In a bread dough it is the difference between slack and stiff.
In a pastry it decides whether the thing holds together at all.

**One gram of flour is one gram of flour.** Weight has no such ambiguity, and
that is the entire case for a scale.

## What varies and what does not

| Ingredient | Volume reliability | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flour | Terrible | Up to 40 percent. Always weigh |
| Brown sugar | Terrible | "Packed" is not a defined quantity |
| Cocoa, powdered sugar | Terrible | Compressible and clumping |
| Chopped vegetables | Poor | Depends entirely on cut size |
| Grated cheese | Poor | Fine or coarse grate changes it enormously |
| Kosher salt | Poor | Brand-dependent by roughly 2x. See below |
| Granulated sugar | Fair | Non-compressible, fairly consistent |
| Rice, grains, beans | Fair | Consistent enough for most cooking |
| Water, milk, stock | Excellent | 1 ml is 1 gram, close enough |
| Oil | Good | About 0.92 g per ml. Fine for cooking |
| Table salt, baking powder, soda | Good at small scale | A teaspoon is genuinely a teaspoon |

The salt row is worth stopping on, because it is the most common invisible recipe
failure. Kosher salt crystal size varies by brand, and by a lot: Diamond Crystal
crystals are large and hollow, so a tablespoon weighs around 8 to 10 grams.
Morton kosher is denser, and the same tablespoon weighs about 15 to 16 grams.
Table salt is denser still, near 18 to 20.

Follow a recipe written for Diamond Crystal using Morton by volume and you have
nearly doubled the salt. This is the origin of a large fraction of "this recipe
was way too salty" complaints, and neither the recipe nor the cook was wrong.
See `flavor.salt`.

## When precision is load-bearing

Precision matters when there is a **chemical or structural reaction with a narrow
tolerance** and — crucially — **when you cannot taste and adjust as you go.**

That second condition is the real dividing line. In a stew you can taste at any
point and correct. In a cake you find out forty minutes later, after the reaction
has finished, and there is nothing to do about it.

**Weigh for:**

- **Baking.** Flour ratios, leavening, hydration. All of it.
- **Doughs of any kind.** Bread, pasta, pastry.
- **Brines and cures.** Salt concentration by weight of water is the only
  meaningful specification, and a cure that is wrong is a safety issue as well as
  a flavor one. See `prep.brines-cures`.
- **Dry brining meat.** Roughly 1 percent of the meat's weight in salt. You cannot
  eyeball a percentage of an unknown weight.
- **Fermentation.** Brine percentage decides which microbes flourish. This is a
  safety matter.
- **Custards and emulsions.** Ratios of egg to liquid have narrow windows.
- **Candy and sugar work.** Concentration is everything.
- **Anything you intend to repeat exactly.** You cannot reproduce what you did not
  record.

**Do not weigh for:**

- Sauteing vegetables.
- Building a soup, stew, or braise.
- Sauces you taste as you build them.
- Seasoning, ever. Salt goes in to taste, not to specification.
- Salads and dressings — the ratio is a starting point you then adjust.
- Anything where you can correct the result before serving it.

The general form: **if you can taste it and fix it, measuring is optional. If the
reaction finishes before you find out, measure.**

## The cost of measuring

There is one, and recipe culture tends to hide it.

Measuring everything trains you to follow rather than to judge. A cook who scales
a quarter-teaspoon of salt is not tasting. A cook who measures a tablespoon of
vinegar into a finished sauce is not asking whether that sauce needs vinegar.
Over time this produces someone who can execute recipes accurately and cannot
cook without one, which is the opposite of what this curriculum is for.

Measuring also costs real time and washing up, and in a fast-moving pan those
seconds are the ones during which something burns.

So the goal is not maximum precision. It is **precision where it pays and
judgment everywhere else.**

## Ratios, which beat both

Ratios are how you stop needing recipes for a whole category at once. Learn one
and you have every recipe in the family.

| Preparation | Ratio | By |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vinaigrette | 3 : 1 oil to acid | Volume, then adjust by taste |
| Bread dough | 5 : 3 flour to water | Weight |
| Pasta dough | 3 : 2 flour to egg | Weight |
| Pie pastry | 3 : 2 : 1 flour, fat, water | Weight |
| Roux | 1 : 1 flour to fat | Weight |
| Custard | 2 : 1 liquid to egg | Weight |
| Stock | 3 : 2 water to bones | Weight |
| Brine | 5 to 8 percent salt | Weight of water |
| Dry brine | 1 percent salt | Weight of the meat |

A ratio scales to any quantity, is independent of your equipment, and tells you
what the preparation *is* rather than what one instance of it looked like.
Michael Ruhlman's *Ratio* is the book on this, and it is worth reading early.

**Baker's percentages** are the same idea formalized. Flour is defined as 100
percent and everything else is expressed relative to it. A dough at 70 percent
hydration has 700g of water per 1000g of flour, regardless of batch size. Two
recipes written this way can be compared at a glance, which is impossible when
one is in cups and the other in grams.

## Calibrating your hand

Eyeballing is not the absence of measurement. It is measurement you have
internalized, and it becomes reliable through deliberate calibration rather than
through hoping.

Do this once, with a scale:

1. **Pinch salt** the way you normally would and drop it on the scale. Repeat
   five times. Learn what your pinch weighs — most land between 0.5 and 1 gram.
   Do this again for each salt you own.
2. **Pour oil into a pan** the way you normally would, then weigh it. Most people
   are surprised.
3. **Pour a "splash" of vinegar,** and weigh it.
4. **Chop an onion** and weigh it. Learn what a medium onion actually gives you —
   usually 150 to 200 grams.
5. **Portion a serving of dry pasta** by eye, then weigh it. Standard is 100
   grams. Most people overshoot substantially.

Twenty minutes of this and your hand becomes an instrument with known error bars.
That is what lets you cook fast and still be right.

## Equipment

**A digital scale** that reads to 1 gram, has a tare button, and goes to at least
5 kilograms. This is the single highest-value inexpensive purchase in a kitchen.
A 0.1 gram jeweller's scale is useful only for curing salts and yeast.

**Tare is the feature.** Bowl on scale, press tare, add flour, press tare, add
water, press tare. One bowl, no cups, nothing to wash, and no arithmetic.

**An instant-read thermometer**, which is the other measurement that is not
negotiable. Covered in `found.doneness`.

**Measuring spoons** stay useful for small quantities of leavening and spice,
where a scale reading 1 gram is not precise enough to be helpful.

**Liquid measuring cups** are fine, because liquids do not compress. Dry
measuring cups are the ones to be suspicious of.

## What goes wrong

- **Volume-measuring flour.** The most consequential measuring error in home
  cooking.
- **Substituting salt brands by volume.** Up to double the salt, invisibly.
- **Measuring seasoning instead of tasting.** Trains dependence and produces worse
  food.
- **Not taring.** Dirtying six vessels for one dough.
- **Scaling a recipe by multiplying everything.** Pan size, surface area,
  evaporation rate, and cooking time do not scale linearly. Ingredients do; the
  physics does not.
- **Precision theatre.** Weighing chopped onion for a stew.
- **Never calibrating.** Eyeballing without ever checking against a scale is
  guessing, and stays guessing.
