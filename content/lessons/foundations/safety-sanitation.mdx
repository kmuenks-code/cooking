## Why this is first and why it is short

This is the only lesson in the curriculum that will not make your food taste
better. It is here because the failure modes are severe and the fixes are cheap,
and because almost all of it collapses into four ideas you can absorb in ten
minutes and then stop thinking about.

I am going to be direct rather than alarming. Home kitchens are not restaurants.
You are cooking for a small number of people you know, usually within an hour of
starting, and you are not holding food on a steam table for six hours. Most
professional food-safety training is calibrated for risks you do not have.

What you do have: a very sharp object, a very hot surface, and raw animal
protein touching things.

## The temperature danger zone

Bacteria multiply fastest between roughly 40F and 140F (4C to 60C). Below that
band, growth slows to nearly nothing. Above it, most of the relevant organisms
die.

The critical thing that gets left out: **time in the zone is cumulative.** It is
not reset by putting something back in the fridge. The forty minutes your
chicken spent on the counter this morning and the twenty minutes it sits out
while you prep tonight add up. The rough working limit is two hours total, and
one hour if the room is hot.

This single fact explains almost every storage rule you have ever been given:

- **Thaw in the refrigerator, not on the counter.** Counter thawing means the
  outside sits in the danger zone for hours while the middle is still frozen.
- **Cool leftovers fast and shallow.** A deep pot of hot stew in the fridge can
  sit in the zone for hours at its center. Spread it out or divide it up.
- **Do not "let it come to room temperature" for an hour** unless you have
  decided that hour is worth spending. For a thick steak it sometimes is, and
  the evidence that it helps is weaker than commonly claimed. For chicken it is
  not.

## Cross-contamination is a sequencing problem

The instinct is to think of contamination as a cleanliness problem solved by
scrubbing. It is better understood as a **sequencing** problem solved by
ordering your work.

The rule that covers nearly everything: **anything that touches raw protein must
either be cooked afterward, or washed before it touches anything else.**

In practice that means deciding your order before you start, which is exactly
what the next lesson on mise en place is about:

1. **Prep everything that is eaten raw first** — the salad, the herbs, the
   garnish. Clean board, clean knife, no protein has entered the picture yet.
2. **Prep raw protein last.** Now the board and knife are contaminated, but
   nothing else needs them.
3. **Wash hands, board, and knife** before returning to anything else.

Doing it in this order means one wash instead of four, and no moment where you
are standing with wet hands wondering what you touched. It is faster, not
slower.

Two specifics worth naming:

- **Do not rinse raw chicken.** It does nothing useful and aerosolizes bacteria
  across your sink and counter. This is one of the few places where a widespread
  home practice is straightforwardly harmful.
- **Watch the invisible vectors.** The faucet handle, the fridge door, the towel,
  your phone, the salt cellar you reached into with a wet hand. These are where
  contamination actually travels, not the board you were already thinking about.

## Temperatures worth memorizing

Most of the chart does not matter for home cooking. These do:

| Food | Safe internal temperature | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Poultry, all of it | 165F / 74C | Thighs are better well past this anyway, around 175F, for texture |
| Ground meat | 160F / 71C | Grinding distributes surface bacteria throughout, which is why whole cuts are different |
| Whole-muscle beef, pork, lamb | 145F / 63C plus a rest | Contamination is on the surface, and the surface gets seared |
| Fish | 145F / 63C | Most cooks pull well below this deliberately, and accept the tradeoff |
| Leftovers, reheated | 165F / 74C | Reheat properly rather than warming through |

Notice that whole-muscle cuts and ground meat differ by fifteen degrees. That is
not arbitrary. Bacteria live on surfaces. A whole steak's interior is
essentially sterile, and searing handles the outside — which is why a rare steak
is reasonable and a rare burger is not. Grinding takes the surface and mixes it
through the middle.

There is a real and legitimate gap between these numbers and how experienced
cooks actually cook. Chicken breast at 165F is dry, and many cooks target 155F
and hold it there, because safety is a function of time *and* temperature rather
than temperature alone. That is a defensible informed choice. Make it knowingly
rather than accidentally, and do not make it for anyone pregnant, elderly, very
young, or immunocompromised.

## The injuries you will actually have

Food poisoning is the risk people worry about. Cuts and burns are the ones that
happen.

**Knives.**

- A dull knife is the more dangerous one. It requires more force and slides off
  food instead of biting, and it slides toward your hand.
- Never try to catch a falling knife. Step back and let it land.
- Never leave a knife in a sink full of water. Someone reaches in.
- Carry a knife at your side, tip down, blade back.
- Cut on a stable board. A towel or damp paper towel underneath stops it sliding.

**Heat.**

- Assume every pan handle is hot. Cast iron and any pan that has been in an oven
  will be, and there is no visual difference.
- A dry towel, not a damp one. Water conducts heat far better than air does, and
  a wet towel on a hot handle delivers a steam burn immediately.
- Water in hot oil is the one genuinely dangerous event in a home kitchen. It
  flashes to steam instantly and ejects oil. Dry food before it goes into hot
  fat.
- A grease fire is smothered, never doused. Lid on, heat off. Water will spread
  it violently.

**The one habit worth building:** know where your towel is, and keep your work
surface clear. Most burns happen while reaching over something.

## What to do when you are unsure

The honest answer to "is this still good" is usually that you cannot tell.
Smell and appearance catch spoilage organisms, which mostly make food
unpleasant, but they do not catch the pathogens that make you ill — those are
frequently invisible and odorless.

So the rule is time-based, not sense-based. Cooked leftovers: three to four days
refrigerated. Raw ground meat: one to two days. Raw whole cuts: three to five.
Label things with a date, because you will not remember.

Throw it out. It is the cheapest decision available.
