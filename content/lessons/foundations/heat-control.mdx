## Why the recipe cannot tell you this

"Heat a pan over medium-high heat for three minutes."

Consider what that instruction is assuming. Your burner output. Your pan's
material and thickness. Your pan's diameter relative to the burner. Whether your
stove is gas, electric coil, or induction. Your altitude. Your kitchen
temperature. The recipe author knew none of these things.

I have seen "medium-high" on two stoves produce a difference of well over a
hundred degrees at the cooking surface. The instruction is not wrong so much as
it is untransferable — it describes what the author did in their kitchen, and it
has no mechanism for arriving at the same result in yours.

The pan, on the other hand, will tell you exactly what temperature it is, if you
know how to ask. That signal is the same in every kitchen on earth.

This is why this lesson exists, and why it comes before every cooking method in
the curriculum. Once you can read a pan, the burner number becomes a rough
starting position rather than an instruction.

## Reading a dry pan: the water test

The most reliable test available to you, and it costs nothing.

Flick a few drops of water into the pan. What happens next tells you the
temperature bracket:

| Water does this | Pan is roughly | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sits, spreads, slowly steams away | Below 212F / 100C | Not preheated. Wait. |
| Sputters, breaks into small drops, evaporates fast and noisily | 212F to about 350F | Gentle cooking, eggs, sweating aromatics |
| Forms one or two tight beads that skitter across the surface like mercury | Above roughly 400F / 200C | Searing. This is your sear signal. |

That last behavior is the **Leidenfrost effect**, and it is worth understanding
rather than just memorizing. When the pan is far hotter than water's boiling
point, the underside of the droplet flashes to vapor instantly. That vapor layer
lifts the droplet clear of the metal and insulates it, so it stops boiling away
and instead glides around on its own steam.

Two useful consequences. First, a skittering bead is unambiguous — it looks like
nothing else, and you cannot mistake it. Second, it explains why food added to a
too-hot pan can behave strangely: a wet surface can briefly ride the same vapor
layer instead of making contact.

This test works on stainless and carbon steel. On nonstick, do not preheat dry at
all.

## Reading oil

Once oil is in the pan, it becomes the gauge.

- **Static and thick, pooling slowly when you tilt the pan** — not hot yet.
- **Thin and mobile, running fast, with a visible shimmer or ripple pattern** —
  this is your working range for most cooking, roughly 350F to 400F.
- **First wisps of smoke** — you are at that oil's smoke point. For a hard sear
  with a high-smoke-point oil this is acceptable and brief. Sustained smoking
  means the oil is breaking down and will taste acrid.

The shimmer is real physics, not folklore: oil thins as it warms, so its
convection currents become visible at the surface. You are literally watching the
oil's viscosity drop.

## Reading sound

Sound is the most underused signal in home cooking, and it is the one that works
while you are looking somewhere else.

- **Silence when food hits the pan** — the pan was not hot enough. The food is
  sitting there absorbing heat rather than cooking. Its surface moisture is not
  boiling off, so it will steam and stick.
- **A loud, aggressive, sustained sizzle** — moisture is evaporating rapidly at
  the surface. This is what a sear sounds like, and it should start the instant
  the food lands.
- **The sizzle dropping to a murmur partway through** — the pan's temperature has
  fallen, usually because too much cold food went in. It is now steaming, not
  searing. Either raise the heat or accept it.
- **A crackling that gets sharper and higher** — surface moisture is nearly gone.
  On a sear this often means you are close to flipping.
- **Near-silence again after a long cook** — the food has dried out and the fat
  has rendered. Depending on what you are making, this is either done or
  overdone.

Learning to hear "the sizzle just dropped" from another room is a genuine skill,
and it arrives faster than you would expect once you know to listen for it.

## Smell is the last warning

Smell is a lagging indicator. By the time you smell something, the reaction has
already happened. But it is precise about *what* happened:

- **Garlic going from raw to sweet-fragrant** — it is ready. Move on within
  seconds.
- **Any sharp, acrid, slightly bitter smell** — something has burned. Usually
  garlic or spices, usually already unrecoverable.
- **Nutty, toasty** — Maillard browning is happening. Good.
- **Sweet and deep** — sugars are caramelizing. Good, and close to the edge.
- **Sour or metallic from oil** — the oil is past its smoke point and degrading.

## The thing nobody warns you about: the temperature drop

Here is the mistake that produces grey, stuck, steamed food more than any other.

You preheat the pan correctly. You add the food. **The pan's temperature
immediately collapses**, because cold food absorbs a great deal of energy very
quickly, and its surface water starts boiling and takes latent heat with it.

How far it collapses and how fast it recovers depends on the pan's thermal mass
and on how much food you added — which is exactly the tradeoff from the previous
lesson. A heavy cast iron pan barely notices. A thin stainless pan can drop below
searing temperature and stay there.

Three consequences:

- **Preheat past your target when the pan is light or the food is cold.** You are
  budgeting for the drop.
- **Do not crowd the pan.** This rule is not about space. Every additional piece
  of cold food is more energy drawn out of a fixed supply. Past a certain point
  the pan cannot recover and everything steams in its own released moisture.
  Cooking in two batches is faster than cooking one bad batch.
- **Dry the surface of your food.** Surface water must be boiled off before the
  surface can exceed 212F, and boiling it off consumes an enormous amount of
  energy. A wet surface is the single biggest obstacle to browning.

## Adjust early, not late

The gap between turning a dial and the cooking surface responding depends
entirely on the pan.

- **Induction** responds almost immediately.
- **Gas** responds quickly at the flame, but the pan's mass still lags.
- **Electric coil or glass-top** is slow in both directions, and coils keep
  radiating for a while after you turn them down.
- **Cast iron** lags no matter what heat source is under it.

So: adjust before you need the change, not when you notice the problem. If you
are searing and the smoke is picking up, you are already past. And when you
genuinely need an immediate drop, do not turn the dial — **lift the pan off the
heat.** Nothing on your stove is faster than removing the pan, and it is a
reversible move.

## Practical calibration

Do this once, deliberately, and it pays off indefinitely.

1. Take the pan you use most. Set the burner where you think "medium-high" is.
2. Wait two minutes. Run the water test.
3. Adjust and repeat until a few drops form a single skittering bead.
4. **Note where the dial actually is.** That is your sear setting, in your
   kitchen, in that pan. It may not be where you assumed.

Repeat for the setting that gives you a gentle sputter, which is your sweating
and sauce-simmering position.

Two numbers, five minutes, and most burner-number guessing goes away.

## The failure modes

- **Adding food to a pan that is not preheated.** The most common cooking error
  there is. It causes sticking, steaming, and grey food, and no later step fixes
  it.
- **Adding wet food.** Pat it dry. This costs ten seconds.
- **Crowding.** Two batches beats one bad batch, always.
- **Chasing the dial.** Adjusting up and down repeatedly means the pan is never
  at equilibrium. Make one change and let it settle.
- **Trusting the timer over the pan.** The recipe's four minutes assumed a pan
  you do not have.
