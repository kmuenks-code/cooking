## The two properties that matter

Ignore brands, ignore price, ignore the aesthetics. A pan has two behaviors that
determine what it can do.

**Responsiveness** — how quickly the cooking surface changes temperature when you
change the burner. High responsiveness means you can rescue a sauce by pulling
the heat, or drop the temperature the instant the garlic smells right.

**Thermal mass** — how much heat energy the pan is holding, and therefore how
little its temperature drops when cold food lands on it. High mass means you can
put a cold steak in and the surface stays hot enough to brown.

These two work against each other. A pan that changes temperature quickly, by
definition, also loses temperature quickly when food lands. A pan that shrugs off
a cold steak also shrugs off you turning the burner down.

Everything below is a different point on that tradeoff.

## Why food sticks

Worth getting out of the way, because it drives most pan frustration and most of
it is not the pan's fault.

Proteins form chemical bonds with bare metal. When a piece of chicken hits a
stainless pan, the proteins on its surface bond directly to the steel. As the
surface browns and dehydrates, those bonds break down and the food releases on
its own.

Which produces the rule that fixes most sticking: **if it will not release, it
is not ready.** Fighting it with a spatula tears the surface and leaves the crust
behind. Waiting another minute usually gets you the whole crust, intact.

The other half is temperature. Metal at a microscopic level is rough, and a cold
pan lets food settle into that texture and grip. A properly preheated pan, with a
thin film of hot oil filling those irregularities, dramatically reduces contact.
This is what the water-drop test in the heat control lesson is checking for.

Nonstick coatings sidestep the whole thing by presenting a surface proteins
cannot bond to. That is a genuine convenience, and it is also why nonstick pans
brown poorly — you have removed the same intimate metal contact that transfers
heat efficiently and creates fond.

## The materials

### Stainless steel (usually clad, with an aluminum or copper core)

Stainless is a poor conductor on its own. Almost all decent stainless cookware is
therefore "clad" — a layer of aluminum or copper sandwiched inside for
conduction, with steel inside and out for durability and non-reactivity.

- **Responsiveness:** good, and better with thinner cladding
- **Mass:** moderate, depending entirely on thickness
- **Browning:** excellent
- **Fond:** excellent, and this is the real reason to own one
- **Acid-safe:** yes

The default pan for anything that ends in a sauce. Stainless produces the best
fond of any material — those browned bits stuck to the bottom are the foundation
of a pan sauce, and nonstick will not give you them at all.

The complaint that "everything sticks to stainless" is almost always a
preheating problem, not a pan problem.

### Cast iron

Heavy, cheap, and the highest thermal mass in a normal kitchen.

- **Responsiveness:** poor. It takes a long time to heat and a long time to
  change once hot.
- **Mass:** very high
- **Browning:** excellent, especially for thick items
- **Fond:** good
- **Acid-safe:** limited. Long-simmered acidic dishes will strip seasoning and
  can pick up a metallic taste. A quick pan sauce with a splash of wine is fine.

Cast iron is unmatched for a heavy sear, because a cold steak barely dents its
temperature. It is genuinely bad for anything requiring control — you cannot pull
back a cast iron pan quickly, and by the time you notice it is too hot, you are
committed.

One correction to common belief: cast iron does *not* heat evenly. Iron is a
mediocre conductor, and a cast iron pan on a burner will have a hot ring and cool
edges. What it does is *hold* heat. Preheat it slowly and give it time to
equalize if evenness matters.

### Carbon steel

The compromise most experienced cooks converge on: lighter than cast iron,
seasons like cast iron, responds faster.

- **Responsiveness:** moderate to good
- **Mass:** moderate
- **Browning:** excellent
- **Fond:** very good
- **Acid-safe:** limited, same caveat as cast iron

The classic restaurant saute pan, and a woks material. If you only want one
searing pan and you do not want to fight nine pounds of iron, this is it.

### Nonstick

- **Responsiveness:** good, usually thin
- **Mass:** low
- **Browning:** poor
- **Fond:** essentially none
- **Acid-safe:** yes
- **Caveat:** coatings degrade, dislike high heat, and do not last

Nonstick is a specialist tool that got marketed as a generalist. It is genuinely
the right choice for eggs, delicate fish, and pancakes — food that is fragile and
that you do not want a crust on.

It is the wrong choice for searing, and using it that way is why people conclude
they cannot brown things. Do not preheat it empty, and do not use it above medium.

### Enameled cast iron

Cast iron's mass with a glass coating: acid-safe, no seasoning, no reactivity.
Heavy and expensive. The right tool for braises and long simmers, and a poor
searing surface compared to bare iron.

## Thickness matters more than material

If you take one practical thing from this: **a thick cheap pan beats a thin
expensive one** for nearly everything involving heat.

A thin pan has almost no thermal mass. Put a cold chicken thigh into a thin
stainless pan and the surface temperature collapses instantly — the pan cannot
supply energy fast enough. The chicken then sits in its own released moisture and
steams. You get grey, sticking chicken, and it is not because the pan is
stainless. It is because the pan is thin.

The same thing happens with a crowded pan, for the same reason: too much cold
mass, not enough stored energy. That is why the "do not crowd the pan" rule
exists, and why it matters far more in a light pan than a heavy one.

## Choosing

| Task | Pan | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sear a steak or chop | Cast iron or carbon steel | Mass keeps the surface hot when cold food lands |
| Sear then build a pan sauce | Clad stainless | Best fond, acid-safe, responsive enough to control the sauce |
| Eggs, delicate fish, pancakes | Nonstick | Fragile food, no crust wanted |
| Sauteing vegetables | Stainless or carbon steel | Browning matters, and you need to move food around |
| Long braise | Enameled cast iron | Mass for stability, enamel for acid tolerance |
| Reducing an acidic sauce | Stainless | Non-reactive, and you can see the color |
| Boiling pasta | Whatever is large | Water is the heat medium; the pan is irrelevant |

That last row matters. When water is doing the work, none of this applies. Save
the thinking for when the pan surface is in direct contact with the food.

## The failure modes

- **Thin pan, cold food, high heat.** The temperature collapses, the food steams,
  and you blame the technique.
- **Preheating nonstick empty on high.** Damages the coating and, at sufficient
  temperature, releases fumes. Never do it.
- **Trying to control cast iron.** By the time you notice it is too hot, you are
  a minute from smoke. Adjust cast iron early or not at all.
- **Fighting food that will not release.** It is telling you it is not ready.
- **Deglazing a seasoned cast iron pan with a lot of wine and simmering it for an
  hour.** That is what stainless and enamel are for.
