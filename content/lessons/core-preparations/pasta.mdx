## The water is an ingredient

Pasta cooking water goes cloudy because starch granules on the pasta's surface
absorb water, swell, and burst, releasing long amylose and amylopectin molecules
into the pot. By the time the pasta is done, that water holds a meaningful
concentration of dissolved starch, and it is salted.

Those starch molecules do two things in a pan.

**They thicken**, which is obvious and is the smaller half.

**They emulsify**, which is the part that matters. A sauce with fat in it — olive
oil, butter, rendered pork fat, cream — and water in it will separate, because fat
and water do not mix. Left alone you get oil floating on a thin liquid, which is
what a lot of home pasta looks like in the bottom of the bowl.

Dissolved starch stabilizes that mixture. The long molecules position themselves
at the boundaries between fat droplets and water, physically preventing the
droplets from finding each other and merging. Add agitation — tossing, stirring —
and you break the fat into small droplets that the starch then keeps separated.

The result is a genuine emulsion: glossy, slightly thickened, and **clinging to
the pasta instead of sliding off it.**

This is the same mechanism as `sauce.monte-au-beurre` and, at greater distance,
as mayonnaise. Starch is doing the job that egg yolk does in one and milk
phospholipids do in the other.

## Finishing in the pan

The technique, and it is not optional if you want the result above.

1. **Salt the water properly** — around 1 to 2 percent by weight, roughly 10 to
   20 grams per liter. See `heat.boiling-simmering`. This is the only chance to
   season the pasta itself.
2. **Have the sauce hot in a wide pan** before the pasta is done. Wide matters:
   you need surface area to toss in and to evaporate.
3. **Pull the pasta early.** Two to three minutes before the box says. It will
   finish in the sauce, and pasta that entered the pan already done will be soft
   by the time the sauce comes together.
4. **Reserve water before draining.** A mug of it. Everyone forgets this once and
   only once. Better still, lift the pasta out with tongs or a spider and leave
   the pot alone entirely.
5. **Pasta into the sauce**, not sauce onto the pasta.
6. **Add a splash of water and raise the heat.** Toss or stir constantly and
   vigorously. Agitation is doing real mechanical work here.
7. **Watch for the change.** Over 30 to 90 seconds the sauce goes from loose and
   watery to glossy and clinging. It is visible. Once you have seen it once you
   will always look for it.
8. **Adjust.** Too thick, more water. Too loose, keep going and let it reduce.
9. **Off the heat for the finish.** Cheese, butter, delicate herbs, a drizzle of
   oil. Parmesan added over direct heat can seize and go stringy.
10. **Serve immediately.** The emulsion is temporary and it will break as it
    cools.

The most common way this fails is not enough heat and not enough movement. It is
an active thirty to ninety seconds, not a gentle stir.

## Al dente, and why

Al dente pasta has a firm core that offers resistance. It is not merely a texture
preference.

Cooked-through pasta has fully gelatinized starch all the way to the center and
has absorbed as much water as it can. Put it in a pan with sauce and it absorbs
nothing more, softens further, and turns to mush.

Pasta pulled underdone still has an ungelatinized core. In the pan it continues
cooking, and it absorbs **sauce** rather than water. That means it finishes
seasoned all the way through rather than seasoned only on the outside — and it
also thickens the sauce as it takes liquid up.

So pulling early is not a garnish on the technique. It is what makes the technique
work.

## Fresh versus dry

These are different ingredients, not different quality levels, and the widespread
belief that fresh is better is simply wrong for a large share of dishes.

| | Dry | Fresh egg |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flour | Durum semolina, high protein | Soft wheat or 00, often lower |
| Liquid | Water | Egg |
| Texture cooked | Firm, springy, real bite | Soft, silky, tender |
| Holds al dente | Yes, easily | Barely. Window is under a minute |
| Cook time | 8-12 min | 60-180 seconds |
| Suits | Oil-based, tomato, seafood, robust sauces | Butter and cream, rich ragu, delicate fillings |
| Best with | Anything that needs the pasta to hold up | Anything that wants the pasta to yield |

Dry semolina pasta is the correct choice for cacio e pepe, aglio e olio, most
tomato sauces, and anything where you want bite. Fresh egg pasta is correct for
tagliatelle with butter, a long-cooked ragu, and filled shapes. A good dry pasta
outperforms mediocre fresh pasta at almost everything.

**Bronze-die versus Teflon-die** is a real distinction within dry pasta. Extruding
through bronze produces a rough, chalky surface; Teflon produces a smooth, glassy
one. Rough surface means more area for sauce to grip and more starch released into
the water. It costs more and it is one of the few pasta upgrades you can taste
directly.

## Shape and sauce

The conventional pairings are not arbitrary, and the logic behind them
generalizes. Two variables: **surface texture** and **whether the shape has
somewhere for sauce to go.**

| Sauce | Shape | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thin oil-based (aglio e olio) | Long thin - spaghetti, spaghettini | Coats a surface. Nothing to catch |
| Light tomato | Long - spaghetti, linguine | Same |
| Chunky vegetable or meat | Tubes and shells - penne, rigatoni, shells | Chunks need somewhere to lodge |
| Rich long-cooked ragu | Wide flat - pappardelle, tagliatelle | Broad surface carries a heavy sauce |
| Cream | Ridged or twisted - fusilli, farfalle | Ridges hold cream against gravity |
| Cheese-and-water emulsion | Long - spaghetti, bucatini | Coating job |
| Brothy or soupy | Small - ditalini, orzo, stelline | Eaten with a spoon |
| Baked | Large tubes - ziti, rigatoni, lasagne | Structure survives the oven |
| Seafood, delicate | Long thin - linguine, spaghetti | Will not overwhelm |

The generalization: **chunky sauces want shapes with cavities; smooth sauces want
shapes with surface area.** That is enough to reason your way to a pairing you
have never seen written down.

## Fresh pasta, briefly

`ingr.flour-gluten` is the prerequisite because fresh pasta is a gluten problem
first.

Rough ratio: 100g flour per large egg, per person. Adjust by feel — egg size and
flour absorbency both vary. It should be stiff, not tacky.

Knead 8 to 10 minutes. You want real gluten development, because the network is
what stops the pasta disintegrating in boiling water.

**Rest at least 30 minutes**, wrapped, at room temperature. Non-negotiable. An
unrested dough springs back and tears when you roll it, and no amount of force
helps. See the resting section of `ingr.flour-gluten`.

Roll thin — thinner than looks right, since it swells. Cut, then either cook
immediately or dry it. Two to three minutes maximum in boiling water, and start
tasting at ninety seconds.

Semolina for dusting, not flour. Flour turns gummy on the surface and clouds the
water; semolina stays granular and keeps the strands apart.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sauce pools at the bottom of the bowl | Never emulsified. Sauced in the bowl | Finish in the pan with water and heat |
| Sauce broke, oil floating | Not enough starch, or not enough agitation | More pasta water, toss harder |
| Pasta bland | Water not salted | 1-2 percent by weight. Only chance |
| Mushy | Cooked through before it hit the pan | Pull 2-3 minutes early |
| Sticky and clumped | Not enough water, or not stirred at the start | More water, stir the first minute |
| Sauce too thick and pasty | Too much starch, too little liquid | Splash more water |
| Parmesan went stringy | Added over direct heat | Off the heat, always |
| Fresh pasta fell apart | Under-kneaded | Real gluten development |
| Fresh pasta tore when rolling | Not rested | 30 minutes, wrapped |
| Forgot the pasta water | Everyone does, once | Lift with tongs instead of draining |
| Rinsed the pasta | Washes off the surface starch | Never rinse, unless for a cold salad |
| Oil in the boiling water | Coats the pasta, sauce cannot adhere | Do not. Stirring is the fix for sticking |
