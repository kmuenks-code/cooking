## The same idea everywhere

Nearly every cuisine that developed slow-cooked savory food arrived independently
at the same construction: **a few aromatic vegetables, cut small, cooked gently in
fat until soft and sweet, as the foundation for everything that follows.**

The vegetables differ. The fat differs. The technique does not.

| Tradition | Base | Vegetables | Fat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| French | Mirepoix | Onion, carrot, celery (2:1:1) | Butter or oil |
| Italian | Soffritto | Onion, carrot, celery, finer cut | Olive oil |
| Spanish | Sofrito | Onion, garlic, tomato, pepper | Olive oil |
| Cajun and Creole | The trinity | Onion, celery, green bell pepper (2:1:1) | Oil, butter, or lard |
| German | Suppengrun | Leek, carrot, celeriac | Butter |
| Polish | Wloszczyzna | Leek, carrot, celeriac, parsley root | Butter or oil |
| Chinese | — | Ginger, garlic, scallion | Neutral oil |
| Indian | Tarka or bhuna base | Onion, ginger, garlic, chile, spices | Ghee or oil |
| Thai | Curry paste | Galangal, lemongrass, shallot, chile, garlic | Coconut oil or cream |
| West African | — | Onion, tomato, pepper, often ginger | Palm or peanut oil |
| Latin American | Recaito | Onion, garlic, cilantro, aji peppers | Oil |

Read that table as one technique with twelve settings, not twelve things to
memorize. The moment you see it that way, the substitution becomes a tool: **the
base is one of the most efficient levers you have for moving a dish between
cuisines**, and you can operate it deliberately.

Take a braise. On mirepoix in butter with thyme and white wine it is French. On
soffritto in olive oil with tomato and rosemary it is Italian. On the trinity in
oil with paprika and cayenne it is Louisiana. On onion-ginger-garlic in ghee with
garam masala it is North Indian. Same cut of meat, same method, same pot.

## What the slow cooking actually does

Three things happen, and none of them are fast.

**Sharpness leaves.** The volatile sulfur compounds in alliums — the ones you get
from cutting, covered in `flavor.aromatics-alliums` — evaporate and break down
with heat. Under-cook the base and that raw sharpness stays in the dish. It does
not cook out later. A braise that has simmered three hours on an under-cooked base
still tastes faintly of raw onion, and nobody can identify why it is off.

**Sweetness arrives.** Onions and carrots hold a lot of sugar. As water leaves and
cell walls break down, that sugar becomes available and concentrated.

**Flavor moves into the fat.** Many aroma compounds in aromatics are fat-soluble
and not water-soluble. The fat extracts them and then distributes them through
the entire dish, since it will coat everything. This is why the base is cooked in
fat rather than simmered in water, and it is not a substitutable detail.

## Sweat or brown

The one real decision, and it determines the character of everything built on
top.

**Sweating** — medium-low heat, a pinch of salt, cooked to translucent with no
color, 8 to 12 minutes. The salt draws water out, and that surface moisture keeps
the temperature at the boiling point of water, which prevents browning. Clean,
sweet, and it lets whatever comes next stay in the foreground.

**Browning** — medium heat, 15 to 25 minutes, taken to golden or deeper. Maillard
and caramelization add depth and complexity, and commit the dish. Better for red
meat braises, dark stews, anything that wants weight.

| | Sweated | Browned |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time | 8-12 min | 15-25 min |
| Color | Translucent, none | Golden to deep brown |
| Flavor | Clean, sweet, transparent | Deep, complex, assertive |
| Salt | At the start, to draw water | After color begins |
| Right for | Tomato sauce, fish, chicken, vegetable soups, delicate braises | Beef braise, ragu, dark stews, French onion |
| Wrong for | Anything wanting weight underneath | Anything meant to taste fresh |

Neither is more advanced. But a dish where you wanted brightness and browned the
base cannot be corrected, and vice versa. Decide before the heat comes on.

## Cut size

The base is not meant to be noticed in the finished dish, so cut size is set by
how completely it needs to disappear and by how long the dish will cook.

| Dish | Cut | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long braise, 3+ hours | 1/2 inch dice | It will collapse regardless |
| Weeknight sauce, 30-60 min | 1/4 inch dice | Needs to break down inside that window |
| Ragu, meant to be invisible | 1/8 inch, near brunoise | Should read as texture, never as vegetable |
| Stock | Rough chop | Strained out. Precision is wasted labor |
| Base you will puree | Rough chop | The blender does not care |

Soffritto is cut noticeably finer than mirepoix, and that is a real difference,
not a stylistic one — it is meant to dissolve entirely into the sauce, and coarse
pieces would show up as identifiable carrot in something that should read as one
smooth thing.

Whatever size you choose, be consistent about it. `knife.uniform-cuts` is
prerequisite here for a reason: a base with mixed sizes gives you scorched
fragments and crunchy chunks in something that should be uniformly soft.

## Building one

1. **Cut everything before the heat goes on.** Once it starts you cannot leave it.
2. **Fat into a heavy pan over medium-low.** Enough to coat generously — a base is
   not the place to economize on fat, because the fat is the delivery vehicle.
3. **Onion first, on its own, with a pinch of salt.** It has the most water and
   takes the longest. 5 to 8 minutes to translucent.
4. **Carrot and celery next.** Another 5 to 10 minutes. Carrot is dense and slow;
   celery gives up water fast.
5. **Garlic, ginger, chile if used, near the end.** 30 to 60 seconds. This is the
   burn point.
6. **Ground spices last,** 20 to 30 seconds in the fat before liquid. See
   `flavor.spices`.
7. **Tomato paste, if used,** 30 to 60 seconds until it darkens to brick.
8. **Then, and only then, liquid.** Adding liquid ends all browning permanently.

The pan should sizzle steadily throughout. If it goes quiet or you see visible
steam, the pan is crowded or the heat is too low, and you are stewing rather than
sweating. Raise the heat or split the batch — same evaporation budget as
`heat.saute`.

## It takes longer than you think

This is the honest part, and the reason most home bases are under-cooked.

Recipes routinely say "cook the onion until softened, about 5 minutes." Five
minutes gets you an onion that is warm and still sharp. Genuinely sweated onion
takes 8 to 12. A properly browned base takes 20 to 25. Caramelized onions, which
recipes claim take 10 minutes, take 40 to 50.

The base is where a dish's depth comes from, and it is the part people rush
because nothing seems to be happening. Nothing seeming to happen is the process.

## Make it ahead

The strongest argument for treating this as a component rather than a step:
**aromatic bases freeze perfectly.**

Make four or six times what you need. Cook it properly, without rushing, on a day
when you have the time. Cool it, portion it into an ice cube tray or a muffin tin,
freeze, then bag the cubes.

A weeknight braise or sauce now starts twenty-five minutes ahead. Drop the frozen
cubes straight into the hot pan — no thawing — and proceed. This single habit does
more for weeknight cooking quality than almost anything else, because it moves the
step you would have skipped into a day when you would not have.

Keep them labelled. Mirepoix and sofrito look identical frozen and are not
interchangeable.

## What goes wrong

- **Under-cooking it.** The dominant failure. Raw allium sharpness stays in the
  finished dish forever.
- **Heat too high while sweating.** You wanted no color and now the dish is
  committed.
- **Garlic in with the onion.** Bitter. See `flavor.aromatics-alliums`.
- **Crowding the pan.** Stewing, not sweating. Nothing sweetens.
- **Not enough fat.** The fat is what carries the flavor into the dish.
- **Uneven cuts.** Burnt fragments and crunchy chunks in the same spoonful.
- **Liquid added too early.** Browning window closed, permanently.
- **Neat dicing for stock.** It gets strained. Rough chop and move on.
- **Sweet onions in a long braise.** Too much water, not enough sulfur to become
  depth.
