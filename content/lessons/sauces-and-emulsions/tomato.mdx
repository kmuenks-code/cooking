## Time is the variable

Put the same pot on the same flame and taste it every fifteen minutes and you are
tasting different sauces.

| Time | Character | Right for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10-15 min | Bright, fruity, acidic, loose. Tastes of raw tomato | Summer pasta, pizza, fish, anything fresh |
| 20-30 min | Balanced. Acidity softened, some body | The weeknight default |
| 45-60 min | Deeper, sweeter, thicker, darker | Baked pasta, richer dishes, meat |
| 90+ min | Jammy, savory, barely fruity, brick-colored | Ragu base, lasagne, braising liquid |

Three things drive this. **Water leaves**, so everything concentrates.
**Acidity drops**, because volatile acids evaporate and the sugars become
relatively more prominent. And **Maillard and caramelization** begin at the edges
of the pot where sauce contacts hot metal, adding browned flavors that were not
there.

The practical consequence: **decide what you are making before you start, not
when you get bored.** Most home tomato sauce is cooked for a duration set by when
the pasta happened to be ready, which is not a decision at all.

Note also that pizza sauce is traditionally **not cooked** — raw crushed tomato,
salt, maybe oil and garlic. It cooks in the oven under the cheese. A pre-cooked
sauce cooked again on a pizza has gone past bright and into muddy.

## Buying tomatoes

Canned beats fresh for sauce most of the year, and this is not a compromise.
Canning tomatoes are picked ripe and processed immediately; supermarket fresh
tomatoes are picked green to survive shipping and never develop sugar or flavor.

An August tomato from a garden or a good market makes a better sauce than a can.
A February supermarket tomato does not, and it is not close.

| Form | What it is | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole peeled | Best fruit, least processed. Crush by hand | The default. Texture control is yours |
| Crushed | Machine-crushed, often with puree added | Convenient. Texture decided for you |
| Diced | Treated with calcium chloride to hold shape | Stays in cubes, resists breaking down. Often not what you want |
| Puree | Cooked and strained | Smooth sauces, no texture |
| Passata | Uncooked strained tomato | Excellent. Smooth without the cooked note |
| Paste | Heavily reduced and concentrated | Umami and body. Not a sauce base |
| Sun-dried | Dried, concentrated | Chopped in as an accent |

**Buy whole peeled and crush them yourself.** The best fruit goes into whole
peeled cans, and you control how chunky the result is. Diced tomatoes are the trap
— the calcium chloride firming agent that keeps them in neat cubes also keeps them
from breaking down into sauce, so they stay as identifiable pieces however long
you cook them.

San Marzano is genuinely good — lower acidity, fewer seeds, denser flesh — but the
name is widely misused. A real DOP designation means something; the words "San
Marzano style" mean nothing. A good non-San-Marzano can beats a fraudulent one.

## The acidity problem

Tomatoes are acidic, around pH 4.3 to 4.9, and the reflexive fix is sugar.

Sugar is usually the wrong answer. It does not reduce acidity — it masks it by
adding a competing taste — and it moves the sauce toward tasting like ketchup. It
is also the least reversible correction, since removing sugar is impossible.

Better options, roughly in order of how often they are right:

1. **Cook it longer.** Volatile acids evaporate. This is free and it is the most
   common correct answer.
2. **Add fat.** Olive oil or butter coats the palate and physically blunts the
   perception of acidity, while carrying aroma. Marcella Hazan's famous sauce —
   tomatoes, an onion halved, and a lot of butter, simmered 45 minutes — works
   almost entirely on this principle.
3. **Add salt.** Salt suppresses the perception of both acidity and bitterness.
   Under-salted sauce reads as sharper than it is. Check salt before anything
   else. See `flavor.salt`.
4. **Add umami.** Depth gives the palate something else to attend to. Parmesan
   rind, anchovy, tomato paste. See `flavor.umami`.
5. **Carrot.** Grated into the base, it contributes sweetness that arrives with
   vegetable flavor rather than as sugar. This is why carrot is in soffritto.
6. **A pinch of baking soda.** Genuinely neutralizes acid rather than masking it —
   an actual chemical reaction. It will foam. Use very little, a quarter teaspoon
   for a large pot, and taste, because too much makes the sauce taste flat and
   soapy. A real tool, and an easy one to overdo.
7. **Sugar, last.** Sometimes correct, especially with poor winter tomatoes. Half
   a teaspoon, not a tablespoon.

Work down that list rather than starting at the bottom.

## Building one

The base sauce, from which everything else is a variation.

1. **Aromatic base.** Onion, or onion and garlic, or a full soffritto, in olive
   oil. Sweated, not browned, if you want the sauce to taste fresh. 8 to 12
   minutes. See `prep.aromatic-bases`.
2. **Garlic near the end** of that, 30 to 60 seconds. Not with the onion.
3. **Bloom tomato paste** if using — 30 to 60 seconds until it darkens to brick
   and sticks slightly. Large return for half a minute.
4. **Tomatoes in.** Crush whole peeled by hand as they go, or in the can with your
   fingers. Rinse the can with a little water and add that too.
5. **Salt now**, lightly. It will concentrate as the sauce reduces, so under-salt
   deliberately and correct at the end.
6. **Hardy herbs now** — a bay leaf, a sprig of oregano or rosemary — since they
   need time. Delicate herbs go in at the very end.
7. **Umami now** if you want it. A parmesan rind dropped in is the easiest version
   and it is transformative.
8. **Simmer, uncovered or partly covered**, at a bare simmer. Not a boil — a
   boiling tomato sauce spits and scorches on the bottom, and scorched tomato is
   bitter. Stir occasionally and scrape the bottom.
9. **Cook to your target time.** Fifteen, thirty, sixty, ninety.
10. **Finish off the heat.** Basil torn in, a slug of raw olive oil, butter if it
    needs richness. Taste and correct salt last, after all the reduction is done.

## Steering it

One base, many destinations. Each of these is a modification of the same pot.

| Destination | Change |
| --- | --- |
| Arrabbiata | Chile flakes bloomed in the oil at the start. No onion, garlic only |
| Puttanesca | Anchovy melted into the oil, olives, capers. No sugar ever |
| Amatriciana | Render guanciale or pancetta first, build the sauce in that fat |
| Marinara | Garlic only, no onion. Fast — 20 minutes. Meant to taste fresh |
| Vodka sauce | Add cream and a splash of vodka. The alcohol carries aroma compounds fat and water cannot |
| Ragu | Brown meat first, longer cook, add wine and a little milk or cream |
| Pizza | Do not cook it at all. Raw crushed tomato, salt, oil |
| Shakshuka | More pepper and cumin, eggs poached in at the end |
| Braising liquid | Cook long and thick, then thin with stock |

## The three-ingredient argument

Marcella Hazan's tomato sauce is a can of whole peeled tomatoes, five tablespoons
of butter, and one onion peeled and halved — simmered 45 minutes, onion removed
and discarded, salt to taste. No garlic. No herbs. No oil.

It is worth making early, because it makes a point that a long ingredient list
obscures: **most of what a tomato sauce needs is time, fat, and salt.** The onion
flavors the sauce without ever being in it. The butter handles acidity and body.
Forty-five minutes handles the rest.

If your elaborate sauce is not clearly better than this one, the additions were
not helping.

## Fresh tomatoes, when they are worth it

In season, with genuinely ripe fruit:

- **Peel them** if you want a smooth sauce, since the skins curl into tough
  fragments that never soften. Score an X, blanch 30 seconds, shock, and the peel
  slips off — a direct application of `heat.boiling-simmering`.
- **Or do not peel**, for a rustic sauce, and accept the texture.
- **Seeds and gel are not bitter**, contrary to common advice — the gel around the
  seeds is where a large share of the glutamate lives, so discarding it discards
  umami. Leave it in.
- **Expect a longer cook.** Fresh tomatoes hold much more water than canned. Twice
  the time is normal.
- **Never refrigerate them beforehand.** Below about 55F, the enzymes that produce
  tomato aroma shut down permanently. A refrigerated tomato does not recover on
  the counter.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Too acidic | Under-cooked, under-salted, or under-fatted | Longer, more salt, more oil or butter. Sugar last |
| Tastes of can | Tomato paste not bloomed, or cheap tomatoes | Bloom the paste. Buy better cans |
| Bitter | Burnt garlic, or scorched on the bottom | Not fixable. Lower heat, stir the bottom |
| Watery | Not reduced enough, or added liquid | Keep going, uncovered |
| Tastes flat | Missing salt or missing umami | Salt first, then a parmesan rind or an anchovy |
| Tomato pieces never broke down | Diced tomatoes | Whole peeled, crushed by hand |
| Tough skin fragments | Fresh tomatoes, unpeeled | Blanch and peel, or accept it |
| Basil went black and bitter | Added early | Off the heat, torn, at the end |
| Sauce split, oil on top | Too much oil, no emulsification | Toss with pasta and pasta water |
| Muddy and lifeless | Cooked far past its target | Decide the target before you start |
