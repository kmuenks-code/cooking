## Why sauces feel harder than they are

Sauces have a reputation as the technical, intimidating part of cooking. Mother
sauces, derivative sauces, French names, things that break.

The reason they feel that way is that they are usually taught as a list of
recipes to memorize. Fifty sauces, fifty procedures.

They are better understood as **three decisions**:

1. What is the base liquid?
2. What makes it thick?
3. How is it seasoned and balanced?

Every sauce you have ever eaten is an answer to those three questions. Once you
can decompose a sauce that way, the list of fifty collapses into a handful of
mechanisms with different ingredients plugged in — and, more importantly, you can
build one that nobody wrote down.

## The four jobs

Before building anything, name what the dish needs. A sauce can do four things,
and most good sauces do two or three.

**Moisture.** Lean, dry-cooked food needs lubrication. A roast chicken breast, a
pork chop, a grilled fish. This is the oldest reason sauces exist and it is why
gravy is on the table.

**Richness.** Fat, delivered where the dish does not have enough of it. Butter
swirled into a pan sauce, cream in a soup, olive oil over beans. Refer back to
the fat lesson — this is the palate-coating job.

**Seasoning.** Concentrated salt, acid, and umami distributed evenly across
something bland. Vinaigrette on lettuce is almost entirely this: the lettuce
brings texture and freshness, the dressing brings everything else.

**Contrast.** Something the dish is not. Acid against fat, cool against hot,
sharp against mellow, fresh against long-cooked. Chimichurri on grilled beef,
yogurt on a spiced stew, salsa on a rich taco.

And the question people skip: **does this dish need a sauce at all?** A perfect
ripe tomato with salt and olive oil does not. A well-marbled steak, correctly
cooked and rested, arguably does not. Adding a sauce to a dish that is already
moist, rich, seasoned, and varied does not improve it — it muddies it.

Sauce as an obligation produces worse food than sauce as an answer.

## The five thickening mechanisms

This is the part that turns memorization into understanding. There are five ways
to make a liquid thicker, and they produce genuinely different textures.

### 1. Reduction

Boil off water. Everything remaining concentrates — flavor, salt, gelatin, sugar
— and the liquid gets more viscous.

- **Feels like:** glossy, intense, clean. Nothing added, so nothing muddied.
- **Watch out for:** it concentrates salt too, so season at the end. And it
  concentrates bitterness and any off flavors just as faithfully.
- **Examples:** pan sauce reductions, glazes, a tomato sauce cooked down.

### 2. Starch

Flour, cornstarch, arrowroot, or the starch already present in pasta water or a
potato. Starch granules absorb water and swell, physically obstructing flow.

- **Feels like:** substantial, coating, opaque. Can be heavy or gluey if
  overdone.
- **Watch out for:** raw flour tastes like paste. Roux exists partly to cook that
  out. Cornstarch thickens clear and glossy but breaks down if held hot too long
  or if there is much acid.
- **Examples:** roux-based sauces, gravy, cornstarch-thickened stir-fry sauces.

### 3. Emulsion

Suspend fat droplets in liquid (or vice versa) so they cannot separate. Requires
an emulsifier and mechanical force.

- **Feels like:** rich, creamy, glossy, smooth.
- **Watch out for:** emulsions break. Temperature and rate of addition are the
  usual culprits. This is what the later emulsion lessons are entirely about.
- **Examples:** vinaigrette, mayonnaise, hollandaise, butter mounted into a pan
  sauce.

### 4. Puree

Suspended solids do the thickening. Blend vegetables, nuts, bread, or fruit into
the liquid.

- **Feels like:** varies enormously with how finely you blend and whether you
  strain. Can be rustic or silky.
- **Watch out for:** texture is entirely a function of the blend and the strainer.
- **Examples:** romesco, coulis, pureed vegetable soups, many curry bases.

### 5. Gelatin

Collagen from bones and connective tissue converts to gelatin over long moist
cooking, and gelatin gives liquid body and a distinctive mouth-coating quality.

- **Feels like:** silky, lip-sticking, luxurious. Unmistakable once you have had
  it.
- **Watch out for:** you cannot add it late. It comes from hours of cooking bones
  or collagen-rich cuts.
- **Examples:** a proper stock-based sauce, the liquid from a long braise,
  demi-glace.

That gelatin row is the honest answer to why restaurant sauces feel different
from home sauces. It is not technique. It is that they started with a stock that
sets to a jelly when cold. It is also why the pan sauce lesson recommends a good
stock, and why making your own is worth it eventually.

## Decomposing sauces

Run some familiar sauces through the three questions and the pattern becomes
obvious:

| Sauce | Base liquid | Thickener | Seasoning and balance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vinaigrette | Vinegar or citrus | Emulsion | Salt, mustard, sometimes sweetness |
| Pan sauce | Stock plus wine, on fond | Reduction, then butter emulsion | Fond, salt, acid, herbs |
| Bechamel | Milk | Starch (roux) | Salt, nutmeg, sometimes cheese |
| Tomato sauce | Tomato | Reduction plus puree | Salt, sweetness against its acid, aromatics |
| Mayonnaise | Lemon or vinegar plus egg yolk | Emulsion | Salt, acid, mustard |
| Gravy | Pan drippings plus stock | Starch (roux) | Salt, pepper, sometimes acid |
| Pesto | Olive oil | Puree | Salt, garlic, cheese |
| Chimichurri | Oil and vinegar | Loose puree, barely emulsified | Salt, acid, garlic, herbs |
| Braising liquid | Stock and wine | Gelatin plus reduction | Everything that was in the pot |
| Yogurt sauce | Yogurt | Already thick, plus emulsion | Salt, acid, garlic, herbs |

Four columns, ten sauces, no memorization. That table is the entire Sauces module
in outline, and it is what makes designing your own sauce possible rather than
mysterious.

## Balancing a sauce

A sauce is more concentrated than the food it goes on, so its balance is judged
differently.

**Taste it on the food, not alone.** A vinaigrette tasted from a spoon will seem
punishingly sharp and salty. Distributed over a bowl of lettuce it may be exactly
right — or still not enough. A sauce tasted alone is being judged out of context.

**Sauces usually need to be slightly over-seasoned** relative to how you would
season a dish, because they are diluted by whatever they land on.

**Check fat and acid as a pair.** Almost every sauce failure is one of those two
missing. Rich and flat: needs acid. Sharp and thin: needs fat.

**Consider what the dish already has.** A sauce for a fatty pork belly should
lean acidic. A sauce for a lean chicken breast should lean rich. The sauce
completes the plate rather than repeating it.

## The failure modes

- **Making a sauce because the dish should have one.** Ask what job it is doing.
- **Tasting the sauce alone and adjusting to that.** Taste it on the food.
- **Repeating the dish instead of completing it.** Rich sauce on rich food is
  monotony.
- **Over-thickening.** A sauce should coat the back of a spoon, not stand up on
  it. Thick reads as heavy and old-fashioned.
- **Seasoning a reduction early.** It concentrates. Wait.
- **Using a starch sauce where reduction would be cleaner.** Starch is a tool,
  not a default. Reduced sauces taste more like themselves.
- **Expecting body from a thin stock.** If your stock does not gel when cold,
  your sauce will not have that silky quality no matter how long you reduce it.
