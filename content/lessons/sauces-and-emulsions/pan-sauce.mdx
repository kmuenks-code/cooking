## Why this is the payoff lesson

Everything on this path has been building to this.

You learned to read a pan's temperature. You learned why a heavy pan browns and a
thin one steams. You learned what the Maillard reaction needs and what it leaves
behind. You learned that salt seasons from the inside and that acid is what rich
food is usually missing. You learned that fat carries aroma and coats the palate.

A pan sauce uses all of it, in about five minutes, with nothing you did not
already have. It is the technique that most reliably makes people think you can
cook.

And crucially, it is a **template, not a recipe**. Once you have the sequence,
you can build a pan sauce from almost any protein, any acid, and any liquid in
your kitchen — which is exactly the kind of thinking the last module of this
curriculum is about.

## The sequence

Five steps. Memorize the shape, not the ingredients.

1. **Sear** the protein. Remove it, and let it rest.
2. **Sweat** aromatics in the remaining fat, briefly.
3. **Deglaze** with something acidic. Scrape.
4. **Reduce**, usually with stock added.
5. **Finish** off the heat — butter, acid, herbs, salt.

That is it. Everything below is detail on each step.

## Step 1: Sear, and leave the pan alone

Standard searing from the Maillard lesson: dry surface, hot heavy pan, don't
crowd, don't move it, wait for release.

When the protein is done, take it out and rest it on a rack or plate. It will
release juices while resting — **pour those into the sauce at the end.** That is
concentrated flavor and throwing it away is a waste.

Now look at your pan. You should see:

- **Fond** — the dark brown stuck-on material. This is your sauce's flavor base.
- **Rendered fat** — usually more than you want.

**Pour off excess fat, leaving about a tablespoon.** This matters more than it
sounds. Too much fat and your sauce will never emulsify properly; it will
separate into a greasy layer over a thin liquid. This is the most common reason a
pan sauce comes out broken.

If the fond looks black rather than deep brown, stop. Burnt fond makes a bitter
sauce and there is no recovering it. Wipe the pan, use stock, and accept a less
interesting sauce.

## Step 2: Aromatics, briefly

Turn the heat to medium. Add finely chopped shallot, garlic, or both, into the
remaining fat.

Keep this short — thirty to sixty seconds. The pan is still very hot and there is
fond on the bottom that can go from browned to burnt quickly. You want the
aromatics softened and fragrant, not colored.

This step is genuinely optional. A pan sauce with nothing but wine, stock, and
butter is a real sauce.

## Step 3: Deglaze

Pour in a splash of acidic liquid — wine, vermouth, sherry, beer, stock with a
splash of vinegar. Around a quarter cup for a normal skillet.

Then **scrape**, with a wooden spoon or a flat-edged spatula, while it bubbles.

What is happening: the fond is water-soluble. It stuck to the pan because it
dehydrated there. Reintroducing liquid dissolves it back, and mechanical scraping
lifts it. Within about thirty seconds your pan bottom should be clean and your
liquid should be dark.

That transformation is the entire point of the technique. You just converted the
mess in your pan into the most flavorful component of the dish.

**Pull the pan off the heat before you pour**, especially if it is screaming hot.
Cold liquid into a very hot pan spits aggressively, and alcohol can flare.

**Why acidic?** Two reasons. Acid helps dissolve the fond. And the finished sauce
will be rich with butter and reduced stock, so it needs acid for balance —
building it in at this stage means it has integrated by the end rather than
sitting on top.

| Deglazing liquid | Suits | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dry white wine | Chicken, pork, fish | The default. Vermouth is better and keeps for months once opened |
| Dry red wine | Beef, lamb, duck | Can turn a sauce muddy-purple; reduce it hard |
| Sherry or Madeira | Chicken, mushrooms, pork | Nutty depth, very forgiving |
| Beer | Pork, sausage, beef | Watch for bitterness concentrating as it reduces |
| Stock plus a splash of vinegar | Anything, and no alcohol | Perfectly legitimate, not a compromise |
| Cider or apple juice plus vinegar | Pork, chicken | Bring your own acid; juice alone is too sweet |

## Step 4: Reduce

Add your main liquid — usually stock, around half a cup to a cup. Bring it to a
brisk simmer and let it reduce.

This is where the sauce gets its body and its intensity. Water leaves, everything
else concentrates, and if your stock has gelatin in it, that gelatin is what
gives the finished sauce its silky, lip-coating quality.

**Do not season yet.** Reduction concentrates salt as faithfully as it
concentrates flavor. A sauce seasoned at this stage will be too salty by the
time it is thick.

**Judge by eye, not by clock.** Recipes say "reduce by half" but you should be
watching two things:

- **The nappe test.** Dip a spoon, pull it out, draw a finger through the coating
  on the back. If the line holds without the sauce running back together, you are
  there. This is the classical test and it is genuinely reliable.
- **The bubble pattern.** As liquid thickens, the bubbles get smaller, slower, and
  more persistent. The sound also drops in pitch. Once you know the change, you
  can hear when a reduction is close.

Aim slightly thinner than you want. The butter in the next step will thicken it
further.

## Step 5: Finish

The step that turns a reduction into a sauce, and the one with an actual failure
mode.

**Take the pan off the heat.** Or at minimum drop to the lowest possible setting.
This matters — read on.

**Add cold butter, a tablespoon or two, in pieces, swirling or whisking
constantly.** The pan should be swirling continuously until each piece disappears.

This is *monter au beurre*, mounting with butter, and it is a temporary emulsion.
Butter is roughly 80% fat, 18% water, and a small amount of milk proteins and
phospholipids that act as emulsifiers. Swirling breaks the butterfat into
microscopic droplets suspended in your reduction, and those emulsifiers keep them
suspended. The result is glossy, opaque, slightly thickened, and rich in a way
that plain reduced stock is not.

**Why cold butter, and why off the heat:** if the butter melts too fast — which
it will in a hot pan — the fat separates out as a layer instead of dispersing into
droplets. The emulsion never forms and you get a greasy film floating on thin
liquid. Cold butter melts gradually as you swirl, giving the emulsion time to
form. This is the same physics as hollandaise, at lower stakes.

Then, in order:

- **The resting juices** from the protein.
- **Acid** — a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar. Taste first. Usually yes.
- **Fresh herbs**, chopped, off the heat so they stay bright.
- **Salt**, now and not before. Taste, adjust, taste again.

Sauce it and serve immediately. A mounted butter sauce is a temporary emulsion —
it will separate if it sits or if it is reheated. This is a sauce made at the last
possible moment, on purpose.

## Building your own

The template with the slots exposed:

```
fat left in pan
  + aromatic (shallot / garlic / none)
  + acidic deglazing liquid (wine / vermouth / vinegar / beer)
  + body liquid (stock / cream / cider / the braising liquid)
  reduce to nappe
  + cold butter, off heat
  + resting juices
  + acid to taste
  + herbs
  + salt last
```

Some combinations that follow directly from it:

- Chicken + white wine + chicken stock + tarragon + lemon
- Pork + cider + stock + mustard + thyme + cider vinegar
- Steak + red wine + beef stock + shallot + a little butter
- Pork or chicken + sherry + stock + mushrooms sauteed with the aromatics
- Fish + white wine + a splash of cream + dill + lemon
- Chicken + stock + a spoon of miso whisked in + rice vinegar + scallion

None of those need looking up once you have the shape.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greasy, separated, oil on top | Too much fat left in the pan, or butter added too hot | Pour off more fat next time. To rescue: off heat, whisk in a splash of cold stock or water |
| Thin and watery | Not reduced enough, or thin stock | Keep reducing. A gelatin-poor stock will never get silky |
| Bitter | Burnt fond, or a reduced liquid that was bitter to start | Not fixable. Start the sauce clean next time |
| Too salty | Seasoned before reducing, or salty stock | Add unsalted liquid and re-reduce, or add butter and acid to mask |
| Flat despite salt | Missing acid | Lemon or vinegar, off the heat |
| Broke while sitting | It is a temporary emulsion | Make it last. Whisk vigorously off heat to bring it back briefly |
| No fond to start with | Seared in nonstick, or protein was wet and steamed | Stainless or carbon steel, dry surface, hot pan |

## The failure modes

- **Washing the pan after searing.** You threw away the sauce.
- **Leaving all the rendered fat in.** The single biggest cause of a broken sauce.
- **Salting before reducing.** It concentrates.
- **Adding butter to a ripping-hot pan.** It separates instead of emulsifying.
- **Reducing to a syrup.** Too thick reads as gluey and old-fashioned. Nappe, not
  glaze.
- **Skipping the final acid.** The most common reason a technically correct pan
  sauce tastes heavy.
- **Making it early and holding it.** It will break. This is a last-minute sauce.
