## The problem grip solves

Hold a knife the way a hammer is held — all four fingers wrapped around the
handle — and try to make a straight cut. It will wander. Not because you are
unsteady, but because of where your hand is relative to the blade.

A knife cutting through food gets pushed sideways. The food is not uniform: a
carrot has a dense core, an onion has layers of different resistance, meat has
grain. Every one of those pushes the blade off line, and the blade rotates around
whatever is holding it.

If you are holding the handle, the blade is a lever arm sticking out in front of
your grip. Small rotations at your hand become large deviations at the tip, and
you are fighting them with wrist strength.

**The pinch grip moves your hand onto the blade itself.** Thumb on one side,
the side of a bent index finger on the other, just forward of the bolster. Now
you are gripping the thing you are trying to control, with almost no lever arm.
The blade cannot rotate without rotating your fingers, and your fingers resist
that without any conscious effort.

The remaining three fingers curl loosely around the handle. They stop the knife
from sliding forward out of your hand. They are not doing the controlling.

### Why it feels wrong at first

Two reasons, both temporary.

Your hand is closer to the sharp part, which feels like the wrong direction. It
is not — your fingers are on the flat of the blade, above the edge, and they
never travel toward it.

And it will feel less secure for about a week. You are trading a strength grip
for a precision grip, and precision grips always feel less secure until the
small muscles adapt. Push through it. Every professional kitchen in the world
uses this grip, and they are not doing it for the aesthetics.

## The claw

The guiding hand is the one that gets injured, and the claw makes most of those
injuries geometrically impossible.

Curl your fingertips under so they point straight down into the board. Let your
**middle knuckles** be the most forward part of your hand. Your thumb tucks
behind your fingers, never out in front of them.

Now rest the flat of the blade against those knuckles.

The knife is now fenced. It can travel down and it can travel forward, and in
neither direction is there a path to your fingertips, because your fingertips
are behind a wall of knuckle. You can cut without watching your holding hand at
all, which is what makes speed possible later.

Three details do the work:

- **The blade stays in contact with the knuckle.** Not hovering near it. Contact
  is what makes the fence a fence, and it is also how you set cut width — you
  retreat the knuckle by the thickness you want, and the blade follows.
- **The thumb tucks behind.** A thumb sticking out past the fingers is outside
  the fence and is the most commonly cut part of the hand.
- **Fingertips point down, not forward.** Flat fingers are exactly as exposed as
  no claw at all.

### Where the claw actually fails

Not on the long, confident cuts. Almost always at the very end of an ingredient,
when the piece is too small to hold with a claw and you flatten your hand to
pin it.

That last inch is where the cuts happen. Two honest options: stop and put the
end in the stock scraps, or turn the piece flat side down, lay your fingers on
top well away from the edge, and cut down with the tip. Do not claw a piece
smaller than your fingertips are long.

## Board setup

Everything above assumes the board does not move.

**Anchor it.** A damp paper towel or a folded dish towel under the board, flat
and smooth, stops it dead. Wet the towel and wring it out — soaking wet is
slippery again. Silicone mats work. Some boards have rubber feet. Do not skip
this, and do not decide it is unnecessary for a quick cut. A board that shifts
mid-cut is the single most dangerous thing on the counter.

**Size it.** A board smaller than about 12 by 18 inches forces you to work in
the middle of a pile of what you already cut, which means pieces roll under the
blade and off the edge. A big board is a safety feature.

**Height.** Standard counters are 36 inches, sized for someone around five foot
eight. If you are taller you are stooping and your back will tell you within the
hour. If you are shorter you are cutting with your shoulders raised, which
removes the downward force you need and makes you push harder instead. Neither
is trivial to fix — a thick board raises the surface an inch or two, a low stool
or a taller board helps a taller cook — but the first step is recognizing which
problem you have.

**Clear the far edge.** Cut pieces need somewhere to go. If the far half of the
board is full, sweep it into a bowl before you keep going. This is where
`found.mise-en-place` and knife work meet.

## Stance

Square up to the board and you will find your cutting arm has to swing outward
in an arc, because your elbow is in the way of your ribs. That arc is why cuts
come out at inconsistent angles.

Instead, stand with your feet roughly shoulder-width and **turn your body about
forty-five degrees away from the board**, cutting-hand side back. Now your
forearm points straight down the line of the cut. The knife travels along your
forearm, which is a natural motion, instead of across your body, which is not.

Stand close enough that you are not reaching. Reaching costs you downward force
and puts your shoulder into it, which is the other way people end up pushing
too hard.

Cut with your **arm and shoulder, not your wrist.** The wrist should be
comparatively still. If your wrist is doing the work, you will be slow, you will
be inaccurate, and you will hurt after twenty minutes.

## The setup, in order

1. **Wipe the counter dry.** Water under a towel under a board is a moving
   board.
2. **Damp towel down, flat and smooth.** No wrinkles under the board.
3. **Board on top.** Press it once and try to shove it. It should not go.
4. **Bowl for scraps on the far side,** bowl or tray for cut pieces beside it.
5. **Turn forty-five degrees off the board.** Feet apart, weight even.
6. **Pinch grip. Check it.** Thumb and forefinger on the blade.
7. **Claw hand. Check the thumb is tucked.**
8. **Blade flat against the knuckle before the first cut.**

By the fourth or fifth session this collapses into about eight seconds and stops
being a checklist.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cuts wander off line | Gripping the handle, not the blade | Pinch grip |
| Uneven thickness | Blade not riding the knuckle | Keep contact; retreat the knuckle to set width |
| Cut fingertips | Flat fingers or an untucked thumb | Curl down, tuck the thumb behind |
| Cut thumb specifically | Thumb out in front of the fingers | It goes behind, always |
| Board slides | No towel, or a soaking wet one | Damp and wrung out, flat |
| Sore wrist | Cutting from the wrist | Move from the shoulder |
| Sore back | Counter too tall or too short for you | Thicker board, or a stool |
| Injured on the last inch | Clawing a piece too small to claw | Save the end for stock |
| Pieces rolling off the board | Board too small, or far edge full | Bigger board, sweep as you go |
