## The reason this is not about neatness

Heat enters a piece of food from its surface and travels inward by conduction.
How long the center takes to arrive is governed, to a good approximation, by the
**shortest distance from surface to center** — the smallest dimension of the
piece — and it goes roughly as the square of that distance.

Squared. That is the whole lesson.

Double the thickness of a piece and it does not take twice as long. It takes
something in the neighborhood of four times as long.

So a pan holding quarter-inch dice and half-inch dice is not holding food that
will finish a little apart. It is holding food where one group finishes at four
minutes and the other at sixteen, in the same pan, over the same flame. At eight
minutes the small pieces are past done and browning toward bitter while the big
ones are still crunchy.

And there is no fix. You cannot rescue the small pieces by pulling them out —
they are already scorched. You cannot rescue the big ones by cooking longer —
that finishes destroying the small ones. You taste the finished dish and find
burnt fragments and raw chunks, and both were determined before the heat came on.

**Uniform cuts are how you get one outcome instead of a distribution of
outcomes.** That is all uniformity is for.

## Squaring off

Vegetables are round, tapered, and irregular. Cuts want flat faces and parallel
sides. Bridging that gap costs you material, and there is no way around it.

Take a carrot. Trim the ends, then slice a thin plane off one long side so it
sits flat on the board without rocking. Now slice the remaining three sides until
you have a rectangular block.

Two things you get from this, and one you pay.

**Stability.** A round vegetable rolls. A rolling vegetable under a knife is how
people get hurt, and it is also why the first cut of a set is usually the worst
one. A flat face is a safety feature before it is a precision feature.

**A reference surface.** Every subsequent cut is measured against a flat plane
rather than a curve. This is why your onion dice came out uneven before you knew
to angle the cuts radially — the geometry underneath was curved and the cuts were
not.

**And you pay in yield.** On a carrot, squaring costs somewhere around twenty to
thirty percent. That is not waste unless you throw it away. Curved trim goes into
stock, soup, or a stir-fry where shape is irrelevant. Keep a bag in the freezer.

## The sequence

Every rectangular cut is the same three moves at different spacings.

1. **Square off.** Flat faces, parallel sides.
2. **Plank.** Slice the block into flat slabs of the target thickness.
3. **Stick.** Stack a few planks, slice lengthwise at the same spacing. You now
   have square-section sticks — this is the batonnet family.
4. **Dice.** Gather the sticks, cut crosswise at the same spacing. Cubes.

The critical detail: **the spacing is the same at every stage.** Quarter-inch
planks, cut into quarter-inch sticks, cut at quarter-inch intervals, give you
quarter-inch cubes. If your planks were uneven, no amount of care in step three
or four recovers it. Errors compound down the chain, and the plank is where they
enter.

Stack no more than three or four planks at once. Taller stacks slide, and a
sliding stack turns one bad cut into a whole batch of bad sticks.

## The standard sizes

| Name | Dimensions | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Julienne | 1/8 x 1/8 x 2 in | Raw salads, garnish, very fast stir-fry |
| Batonnet | 1/4 x 1/4 x 2-2.5 in | Crudites, fries, roasting sticks |
| Baton | 1/2 x 1/2 x 2.5 in | Heavy roasting, stews |
| Brunoise | 1/8 in cube | Sauces, vinaigrettes, garnish. See `knife.brunoise` |
| Small dice | 1/4 in cube | Mirepoix, most weeknight cooking, soups |
| Medium dice | 1/2 in cube | Stews, braises, sheet-pan roasting |
| Large dice | 3/4 in cube | Long roasts, hearty stews |
| Paysanne | 1/2 x 1/2 x 1/8 in | Thin square tiles for clear soups |

The vocabulary is worth knowing because it is compact and unambiguous, and
because recipes use it. But do not mistake the names for the point. The point is
that you picked a size on purpose.

## Picking a size

Match the size to the time the method gives you.

| Method | Size | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quick saute, 5-8 min | Small dice, 1/4 in | Finishes inside the window |
| Stir-fry, 3-4 min | Julienne or thin bias | Very little time available |
| Soup or stew, 45+ min | Medium dice, 1/2 in | Small dice dissolves entirely |
| Sheet-pan roast, 30-40 min | Medium to large | Surface area for browning, mass to survive it |
| Mirepoix under a braise | Small to medium | Meant to break down and disappear |
| Raw in a salad | Julienne or small | Texture, and you are eating it uncooked |
| Stock | Rough chop | Strained out. Precision here is wasted labor |

The last row is the important one, and it comes back below.

## Mixed vegetables in one pan

This is where the principle earns its keep.

Different vegetables have different densities and different water content, so
identical cuts do not finish at the same time. A half-inch potato cube and a
half-inch zucchini cube are not comparable — the zucchini will be gone before
the potato is done.

Two ways to handle it, and you can use both:

**Cut to time, not to size.** Deliberately cut the denser vegetable smaller. If
potato takes twice as long as zucchini at the same size, cut the potato smaller
until they converge. Uniformity within an ingredient, staggered sizes across
ingredients.

**Stagger the entry.** Same size for everything, but the potato goes in first
and the zucchini joins it partway through. Easier to reason about, and it
generally browns better because nothing sits in the pan longer than it needs to.

Roasting usually rewards the first approach, because everything wants to come
out of the oven at once. Sauteing usually rewards the second, because you are
standing there anyway.

## When not to bother

Knowing where precision stops paying is part of the skill, and it is the part
that separates someone who has internalized this from someone performing it.

**Skip it for:**

- **Stock.** Rough chop. It gets strained out and discarded. Neatly diced stock
  vegetables are a beginner tell.
- **Anything getting pureed.** Soup that meets a blender does not care.
- **Long braises** where the vegetables are meant to collapse into the sauce.
- **Any dish where the vegetable disappears** into a base.

**Do it for:**

- Anything sauteed or roasted, where doneness spread is visible and permanent.
- Anything served visibly whole.
- Anything raw.
- Anything cooked fast, because fast methods have no slack to absorb variation.

The general rule: **precision matters in proportion to how short the cooking
window is and how visible the pieces stay.** Fast and visible, be precise. Slow
and dissolving, do not waste the time.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dice comes out as trapezoids | Never squared off | Square first. There is no shortcut |
| Sticks vary in thickness | Uneven planks | Fix the plank stage; errors compound after it |
| Stack slides mid-cut | Too many planks at once | Three or four maximum |
| Vegetable rolls | No flat face on the board | That is what squaring is for |
| Burnt bits plus raw chunks | Size variation in one pan | This lesson, applied |
| Perfect dice, still uneven doneness | Different vegetables, same size | Cut to time, or stagger the entry |
| Prep takes forever | Precision on things that get strained or pureed | Rough chop and move on |
| Last inch is always ragged | Piece got too small to hold safely | Stop and put the end in the scrap bag |
