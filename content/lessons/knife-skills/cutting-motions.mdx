## Why pressing does not work

Set a knife on a tomato and press straight down. It skids, dents the skin, and
eventually bursts through, and what you get is not a slice.

Now set the knife on the same tomato and draw it backward while pressing gently.
It goes straight in.

The difference is not sharpness. It is the same edge either way. The difference
is that a cutting edge works by **sliding**, not by pressing.

At the scale that matters, even a mirror-polished edge is not a smooth line. It
is a series of microscopic irregularities — a very fine saw. Pressing straight
down engages none of them; the whole edge just pushes on the material at once,
and the material pushes back across its entire contact area. Sliding drags those
irregularities across the surface, and each one concentrates stress on a tiny
area. Stress is force over area, so shrinking the area is the same as multiplying
the force.

There is a second effect stacked on top. A blade moving diagonally through
material presents a shallower effective wedge angle than the same blade moving
straight down — the same reason a road cut into a hillside at an angle climbs
more gently than one going straight up. A shallower wedge separates the material
with less force.

Both effects point the same way, and they give you the rule that governs
everything below:

**Every cut moves the blade forward or backward. Never straight down.**

A cut that goes straight down is not cutting. It is crushing.

## What crushing costs you

This is not a matter of neatness.

- **Flavor changes.** Ruptured cells release enzymes onto substrates they are
  normally kept apart from. In an onion that means more pungency. In garlic it
  means more allicin. In herbs it means the aromatic oils end up on your board
  and oxidizing rather than in the food.
- **Color goes.** Basil turns black at the cut edge, and quickly. That is
  enzymatic browning, and it is a direct readout of how many cells you burst.
- **Texture goes.** A crushed tomato slice weeps. A crushed potato edge goes
  gummy from released starch.
- **It spoils faster.** Ruptured cells leak nutrients that microbes eat.
  Crushed-cut produce genuinely has a shorter fridge life than cleanly cut.

So the motion is not cosmetic. It is upstream of taste.

## The four motions

### Push cut — forward and down

The blade travels **away from you** and downward at the same time, so the edge
enters at the heel and exits at the tip. One smooth stroke, then lift and reset.

This is the workhorse. Firm vegetables, most slicing, anything where you want a
clean face on both pieces.

It is also the motion a **flat-profile knife** — gyuto, santoku, nakiri — is
built for, because it keeps the edge in contact with the board through the whole
stroke.

*Use it for:* carrots, potatoes, celery, squash, most slicing, most dicing.

### Draw cut — backward and down

The mirror image. The blade travels **toward you** and down, entering near the
tip and exiting at the heel.

Use it when the surface resists entry. Pulling backward keeps the edge biting
into the skin rather than skating across it, which is why it is the motion for
tomatoes and for delicate proteins where pushing would tear.

It is also the motion for a long, thin slicing knife, where you want a single
uninterrupted pass rather than a stroke that runs out of blade.

*Use it for:* tomatoes and other tight-skinned produce, raw fish, cooked
proteins, anything where a push cut skids.

### Rock chop — pivoting on the tip

The tip stays planted on the board. The handle rises and falls, and the contact
point rolls along the curved belly of the blade. Your guiding hand feeds material
under it or gathers the pile back together between passes.

This is the fastest way to reduce a quantity of something to small pieces, which
is exactly what you want for herbs, garlic, and rough chopping.

It requires a **curved profile.** On a flat knife, the belly runs out and the
edge lifts off the board partway through the arc, leaving uncut hinges you have
to go back for. If rocking feels bad, check your knife before you blame yourself.

The failure mode is chopping the same pile fifty times because it is satisfying.
Every extra pass ruptures more cells. Gather, make a few decisive passes, and
stop.

*Use it for:* herbs, garlic, nuts, rough chopping, anything going into a pan
where exact size does not matter.

### Tap chop — up and down, tip lifted

The blade comes fully off the board between cuts, lands, and comes up again. The
smallest amount of forward travel, close to a straight press.

This is the exception, and it exists for foods that are so soft they will smear
under any horizontal motion, and for the last stage of very fine mincing where
you want to consolidate a pile without dragging it around the board.

*Use it for:* garlic paste at the end, very soft cheese, blanched garlic,
scallion greens that would otherwise slide.

Because it is closest to pressing, it demands the sharpest edge of the four.
With a dull knife it stops being a tap chop and becomes crushing.

## Choosing

| Food | Motion | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carrot, potato, firm root | Push cut | Dense and uniform, no skin resistance, wants a clean face |
| Tomato, plum, grape | Draw cut | Tight skin. Pushing skids across it |
| Onion (the dice) | Push cut | Layers hold together; the face matters |
| Parsley, cilantro, basil | Rock chop, few passes | Volume reduction, and every extra pass is bruising |
| Garlic | Rock chop then tap chop | Rock to break it down, tap to consolidate |
| Raw fish | Long draw cut | One pass, no sawing, no tearing |
| Cooked roast | Long draw cut | Sawing shreds the fibers |
| Soft cheese, avocado | Tap chop or a wet blade | Any drag smears it |
| Winter squash, hard rind | Push cut from the heel | You need mass and leverage at the back of the blade |
| Anything, with a dull knife | Sharpen first | No motion rescues a dull edge |

## Matching motion to your knife

From `knife.anatomy-selection`: profile decides motion.

| Blade | Rock chop | Push cut | Draw cut |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Curved German chef's knife | Excellent | Good | Good |
| Flatter Japanese gyuto | Poor | Excellent | Good |
| Santoku or nakiri | Poor | Excellent | Fair |
| Long slicer | Not possible | Fair | Excellent |

If you own a flat knife and rocking feels clumsy, the knife is telling you
something true. Switch to push cuts rather than fighting it.

## Sawing is not slicing

The distinction matters and it is easy to blur.

A **slice** is one continuous pass in one direction. A **saw** is many short
strokes back and forth over the same line.

Sawing reverses direction repeatedly, and every reversal drags the edge back
across a surface it has already opened, tearing rather than cutting. It leaves a
ragged face, it ruptures far more cells, and on soft food it destroys the
structure.

If you find yourself sawing, one of three things is true: the knife is dull, the
blade is too short for what you are cutting, or you are using the wrong motion.
All three are fixable and none of them are fixed by sawing harder.

## What goes wrong

- **Pressing straight down.** The root error. Nearly everything below is a
  version of it.
- **Sawing.** Diagnose the cause rather than pushing through.
- **Rocking a flat knife.** Leaves uncut hinges and feels like personal failure
  when it is a geometry mismatch.
- **Over-chopping herbs.** The pile is done long before it stops being
  satisfying to chop.
- **Tap chopping with a dull knife.** The least forgiving motion paired with the
  least forgiving edge.
- **Letting the blade travel too far forward.** Push cuts that overshoot run the
  tip off the front of the board and onto the counter.
- **Changing motion mid-cut.** Pick one before the blade touches the food.
