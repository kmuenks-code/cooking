## A knife is a wedge

Everything else is detail.

You apply force to the spine. The blade concentrates that force onto an edge a
fraction of a micron wide, which is enough pressure to separate the food. Then
the rest of the blade has to push the two halves apart to get through.

That second part is where most knives are won and lost, and it is the part
nobody talks about. The apex — the actual cutting edge — is the easy half. Any
knife can be sharpened to a fine apex. What decides whether a knife feels
effortless or like work is **how much material sits behind that apex**, because
that is what has to wedge the food apart on the way through.

This is why a cheap thin knife often outperforms an expensive thick one, and why
a properly sharpened knife can still feel bad. Sharpness is the apex. Geometry
is the wedge. You need both, and only one of them shows up in the marketing.

## The parts, and what each is for

| Part | Where | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tip | Front inch | Fine work, piercing, scoring. Where precision lives |
| Belly | Curved front half of the edge | The rocking surface. More curve, more rock |
| Heel | Back of the edge, near the handle | Where you put force. Hard vegetables, squash, chicken joints |
| Edge | The whole cutting line | The apex plus the grind behind it |
| Spine | Top of the blade | Where your fingers push. Should be rounded, not square |
| Bolster | Thick collar where blade meets handle | Balance and finger guard, and often a problem — see below |
| Tang | Blade steel extending into the handle | Full tang runs the whole length. Matters less than people say |
| Handle | The part you do not actually grip | See the next lesson. You grip the blade |

Two of those deserve more than a table row.

**The spine.** You will spend a lot of time with an index finger resting on or
near the spine. If the manufacturer left it square and unfinished, it will dig
into your finger within twenty minutes of prep. Run your thumb along it before
you buy. This is a real ergonomic defect that shows up at every price point, and
almost no review mentions it.

**The bolster.** A *full* bolster runs all the way down to the edge. It looks
substantial and it creates two genuine problems. The last inch of edge — the
heel, where you do your heaviest cutting — cannot reach a sharpening stone, so
it goes permanently dull. And the extra steel pulls the balance point back
toward your hand in a way that makes the knife feel less controllable in food. A
*half* bolster stops short of the edge and has neither problem. Prefer half, or
none.

## Hardness versus toughness

This is the tradeoff underneath every knife comparison you will ever read.

Steel hardness is measured on the Rockwell C scale. A typical German kitchen
knife sits around 56 HRC. A typical Japanese one sits around 60 to 63.

Harder steel holds a finer apex and holds it longer. It is also more brittle, so
it chips instead of rolling when it meets a bone or a plate. Softer steel will
not take as fine an edge and dulls sooner, but it deforms rather than fractures,
which means a honing rod brings it back in ten seconds instead of needing a
stone.

| | Softer, around 56 HRC | Harder, around 61 HRC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Edge retention | Shorter | Longer |
| Peak sharpness | Good | Better |
| Failure mode | Edge rolls | Edge chips |
| Recovery | Hone it back in seconds | Needs a stone |
| Tolerance for abuse | High | Low |
| Typical geometry | Thicker, more curve | Thinner, flatter |

Neither column is correct. The right question is what happens in *your* kitchen.
If you cut on wood, avoid bones, and are willing to learn a stone, the hard
column gives you a better knife. If you share the kitchen, cook fast, and
occasionally hit something you should not, the soft column survives it and the
hard one chips.

Hardness and geometry are correlated but not the same thing. Harder steel can be
ground thinner without folding, which is why Japanese knives tend to be thinner
— but you can buy a thin softer knife, and those are often the best value on the
market.

## Profile decides motion

The curve of the edge, seen from the side, determines how the knife wants to be
used.

A **German-style** chef's knife has a pronounced belly. Set the tip on the board
and the edge sweeps up in a long arc. That arc is what makes rock chopping work:
you pivot on the tip and the contact point rolls along the board.

A **Japanese-style gyuto** is flatter, with the curve concentrated near the tip.
Try to rock it and you will find it lifts off the board too early, leaving
uncut hinges behind. It wants a push cut — forward and down — instead.

Neither profile is better. But a knife and a motion have to match, and a lot of
people who believe they are bad at knife work bought a flat knife and are trying
to rock with it. The next two lessons cover the motions; when you get there,
check your knife's profile against what you are asking it to do.

**Santoku** knives are the flattest common option, with a sheep's-foot tip and
usually a shorter blade. Good at straight downward chopping, poor at rocking,
and the blunt tip removes a capability you will eventually want.

## The three knives

Almost all home cooking is covered by three blades, because there are only three
fundamentally different cutting problems.

**A chef's knife, 8 inches.** Ninety percent of your cutting. Eight inches is the
default because it clears a large onion in one pass while still fitting most
boards and most hands. Ten inches cuts faster and intimidates people into not
using it. Six inches is a compromise that is worse at everything than either.

**A paring knife, 3 to 4 inches.** For work done *in your hand*, off the board:
hulling strawberries, peeling a shallot, scoring a chestnut, cutting the core
out of a tomato. The chef's knife is not merely bad at these. It is unsafe at
them.

**A serrated bread knife, 9 to 10 inches.** Serrations work on a different
principle. Each tooth is a stress concentrator, so instead of pressing a
continuous line through a resistant surface, you attack it at points. That is
why a serrated edge goes through bread crust and tomato skin and citrus peel
where a smooth edge skates — and also why serrated knives cannot practically be
sharpened at home, and are effectively consumable.

Buy these three separately. **Do not buy a block set.** A set prices its filler —
six steak knives, the shears, the block itself, the rod — into money that could
have gone into one good chef's knife. You will use three pieces and store the
rest forever.

The fourth knife, when you get there, is a **boning or fillet knife**: narrow,
flexible, made for following bone and separating joints. That is tier 3 of this
module. You do not need it yet.

## What to actually check before buying

Handle the knife if you possibly can. Specifications will not tell you any of
this.

- **Knuckle clearance.** Grip it and set the edge flat on a counter. Do your
  knuckles hit? If they hit the counter, they hit the board on every single cut.
- **The spine, against your finger.** Square edges hurt within the hour.
- **Balance.** Rest the knife across one finger. The balance point should sit at
  or just forward of where blade meets handle. Far back means a heavy bolster.
  Far forward means a blade-heavy knife that is tiring to control.
- **Thickness behind the edge.** You cannot measure this by hand but you can see
  it. Look at the blade edge-on. A blade that thickens sharply just above the
  apex will wedge in food no matter how sharp you get it.
- **Weight.** Heavier is not better. Heavier is better only for driving a rock
  chop through dense food, and worse for everything else.

Price stops buying much above roughly the fifty-to-eighty-dollar mark for a
chef's knife. Past that you are paying for fit and finish, materials, and looks.
A well-chosen inexpensive knife that you actually sharpen will out-cut an
expensive knife that you do not.

## Care, in four rules

- **Never the dishwasher.** Heat, caustic detergent, and knocking against other
  items. It damages the edge, and on high-carbon steel it does visible corrosion
  damage in a single cycle. Hand wash, dry immediately.
- **Wood or plastic boards only.** Glass, stone, marble, and ceramic destroy an
  edge on contact. A knife that goes dull in a week is usually a board problem,
  not a knife problem.
- **Do not store loose in a drawer.** Blade-on-blade contact chips edges, and
  reaching blind into a drawer of loose knives is how people get cut. A magnetic
  strip, an in-drawer tray, or blade guards all work.
- **High-carbon steel needs drying and occasional oiling.** It will patina, which
  is fine and mildly protective. It will also rust if left wet, which is not.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying the set.** The most common and most expensive knife mistake.
- **Buying a full bolster.** Guarantees a permanently dull heel.
- **Blaming the knife for the board.** Glass and stone boards are the fastest way
  to ruin a good edge.
- **Buying a santoku first** because it looked approachable, then finding you
  cannot rock with it and the tip will not pierce.
- **Never sharpening it.** A hundred-dollar dull knife is worse than a
  twenty-dollar sharp one, and it is not close. See `knife.sharpening`.
- **Optimizing steel before geometry.** People agonize over alloys while cutting
  with a blade a quarter-inch thick above the edge.
