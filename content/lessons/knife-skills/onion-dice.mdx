## Why this cut first

You could learn to cut a carrot first. Carrots are easier, more forgiving, and
nothing bad happens if you get it slightly wrong.

That is exactly why we are starting with the onion. The onion is the first
ingredient where the way you hold the knife changes the way the food tastes.
Cut a carrot two different ways and you get two different shapes of carrot. Cut
an onion two different ways and you get two genuinely different ingredients:
one sharp and structural, one sweet and collapsing. Understanding that is worth
more than a tidy dice.

## What is actually inside an onion

An onion is not a solid ball. It is a series of concentric layers, each one a
modified leaf, arranged in arcs around a central core, and held together at the
bottom by the root end. Within each layer, the fibers run vertically, from the
root end to the stem end, like lines of longitude on a globe.

Two things follow from that structure, and nearly everything else in this
lesson is a consequence of one of them.

**First: the root end is the handle.** It is the only part holding the layers
together. Leave it intact and the onion stays assembled while you work on it.
Cut through it and you are chasing loose layers around the board.

**Second: the onion has a grain,** and it runs pole to pole. You can cut along
those fibers or across them, and the two produce different food.

## Why cutting an onion hurts

Onion cells store two things in separate compartments: sulfur compounds, and an
enzyme called alliinase that acts on them. Intact, they never meet. Rupture the
cell and they mix, producing (among other things) a volatile compound that
drifts up, reaches the water on the surface of your eye, and forms a mild acid.
Your eye responds by flushing it out.

This is not a piece of trivia. It is a control mechanism, because it means
**pungency is a function of how many cells you rupture.**

Three practical consequences:

- **A sharp knife cuts cells. A dull knife crushes them.** A dull blade ruptures
  a wide band of cells on either side of the cut instead of severing a narrow
  line. It makes the onion sharper, makes you cry more, and makes the onion
  start breaking down sooner. This is the most concrete argument for sharpening
  you will encounter.
- **Fewer passes means less rupture.** Cutting confidently once beats sawing
  three times, for flavor and not just for speed.
- **Cutting across the grain ruptures more cells than cutting along it.** Which
  brings us to the decision that actually matters.

## The decision: with the grain or across it

Here is the part that most knife tutorials skip.

**Cutting with the grain** (radially, pole to pole, following the fiber
direction) severs relatively few cells. The pieces hold together, taste milder,
and survive long cooking with their shape intact.

**Cutting across the grain** (into rings or half-moons) cuts through every
fiber in the layer. More cells rupture, so the onion tastes sharper raw, and it
falls apart faster under heat.

Neither is correct. They are two tools:

| You want | Cut | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onion you can still see in a finished braise | With the grain | Pieces stay structurally intact through long cooking |
| Onion that melts into a sauce or a soup base | Across the grain | Ruptured cells break down faster and disappear |
| Milder raw onion for a salad or a salsa | With the grain | Less rupture, less pungency |
| Deeply caramelized onions | Across the grain | You *want* collapse; structure is not the goal |

Once you have internalized this, "dice an onion" stops being one instruction and
becomes a question the recipe should have answered and usually did not.

## Why the dice comes out uneven

The standard method is to halve the onion pole to pole, lay a half flat, make a
series of vertical cuts toward the root, then cut across them to release the
dice.

The common failure is that the vertical cuts are made **parallel to each other**,
straight down. Because the onion's layers are arcs rather than flat planes, a
parallel cut hits the outer layers at a steep angle and the inner layers nearly
flat. You get wide pieces from the outside and narrow pieces from the middle,
from a cut that felt perfectly even while you were making it.

The fix is to angle each cut toward the center of the onion — **radially**,
aiming roughly at the root, so each cut crosses the layers at a similar angle.
The dice comes out visibly more uniform, from the same number of cuts and the
same amount of effort.

### And the horizontal cut?

Classical French technique adds one or two horizontal cuts toward the root
before the vertical ones, to control the third dimension of the dice.

There is a real, live disagreement about whether this is worth doing. The
argument against, associated most prominently with Kenji López-Alt, is that the
onion's natural layers already divide the piece in that dimension, so the
horizontal cut adds a genuinely dangerous move (blade traveling toward your
holding hand, inside a slippery onion) for a marginal gain in evenness.

I would skip it while you are learning, and revisit it once the rest of the cut
is automatic. But watch both videos above and form your own view — this is a
good early example of "expert consensus" being thinner than it looks.

## Why evenness matters at all

It is tempting to file uniformity under aesthetics. It is not.

Cooking rate scales with size. In a single pan, at a single temperature, for a
single span of time, small pieces finish while large pieces are still raw. With
onions this shows up fast and unpleasantly: the small fragments hit the pan,
darken, and turn bitter, while the large chunks are still crunchy and sharp.
You then taste the dish and find both bitterness and raw onion in it, and
neither one is fixable.

A uniform dice is how you get one outcome instead of a distribution of outcomes.

## Choosing a size

| Size | Roughly | Use | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fine, 1/8 inch | Brunoise-adjacent | Sauces, dressings, quick pan sauces | Disappears entirely; contributes flavor without texture |
| Medium, 1/4 inch | Standard dice | Mirepoix, braises, soups, most weeknight cooking | Survives long cooking while still softening completely |
| Large, 1/2 inch | Rough dice | Roasts, stews, sheet-pan cooking | Enough mass to sweeten without disintegrating |
| Rough chop | Whatever | Stock | Strained out and discarded; evenness is irrelevant and precision is wasted effort |

That last row matters more than it looks. Knowing when *not* to bother is part
of the skill. Dicing stock onions neatly is a beginner tell.

## The cut, step by step

1. **Trim the stem end** (the pointed top). Leave the root end fully intact.
2. **Halve pole to pole,** through both the stem end and the root.
3. **Peel,** taking the papery skin and the first layer if it is dry or tough.
4. **Lay a half flat side down.** Flat side down. An onion resting on a curved
   surface will roll, and that is where cuts happen.
5. **Make vertical cuts angled toward the root,** radially rather than parallel.
   Stop short of the root end so the halves stay attached.
6. **Cut across those,** perpendicular to the board, and the dice falls away.
7. **Discard the root end** — or keep it for stock.

Spacing between your cuts is what sets dice size, and it is the only variable
you control here. Aim for the same spacing in both directions.

## What to watch for

- **Rolling onion.** Flat side down, always. This is the single most common
  cause of onion-related injuries.
- **Cutting through the root too early.** The half falls into layers and you
  spend the rest of the cut reassembling it.
- **Sawing.** One confident pass per cut. Sawing ruptures more cells, which
  means more pungency and more tears.
- **A dull knife.** If the onion is sliding rather than cutting, or you are
  crying unusually hard, that is the knife, not the onion.
