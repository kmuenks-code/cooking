## Flour contains no gluten

This is the fact everything else hangs on, and it is stated wrong almost
everywhere.

Wheat flour contains two storage proteins: **glutenin** and **gliadin**. Neither
is gluten. Dry, they sit inert in the flour and do nothing at all.

Add water and they hydrate and become mobile. Add mechanical energy — mixing,
kneading, folding, even the slow motion of an autolyse — and they begin to bond
to each other, forming an extended three-dimensional network. *That* network is
gluten, and it did not exist until you made it.

The two proteins contribute different properties, and the balance between them is
what dough feel actually is:

- **Glutenin** provides **elasticity** — the springy quality that pulls dough back
  when you stretch it.
- **Gliadin** provides **extensibility** — the willingness to stretch and stay
  stretched.

A dough that snaps back and will not roll out has too much elasticity relative to
extensibility, or has simply not rested. A dough that tears and will not hold its
shape is the other way around.

You can see the network directly. Make a stiff dough, rest it, then knead it
under running water. The starch washes out as cloudy water and you are left
holding a grey, rubbery mass. That is gluten, isolated. It is the same substance
seitan is made from.

## Why you want it, and why you often do not

Gluten's job is to be a **gas-trapping elastic net.** As yeast produces carbon
dioxide, or as steam forms in a hot oven, the network stretches around each
bubble and holds it rather than letting it escape. That is what makes bread rise
and stay risen.

But the same network is what makes food **chewy and tough.** In bread, chewy is
the goal. In a scone, a muffin, a pie crust, or a cake, it is a defect, and those
are exactly the products where people ruin their results by mixing too long.

Every flour product is a decision about **how much network to build**:

| Product | Gluten wanted | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bread, bagels, pizza | Maximum | Needs to trap gas and hold a chew |
| Fresh pasta | High | Needs to survive boiling without falling apart |
| Pizza | High | Stretch without tearing, chew in the crust |
| Pancakes, muffins, quick breads | Minimum | Chew reads as toughness |
| Pie and tart pastry | Minimum | Flaky and tender, not elastic |
| Cake | Minimum | Fine, soft crumb |
| Biscuits and scones | Very little | Tender, short |
| Sauce thickening | None | You want starch, not structure |

## Flour by protein content

Protein content is the ceiling on how much network you can build. It is printed
on the bag, or derivable from the nutrition panel.

| Flour | Protein | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cake flour | 6-8% | Cakes. Usually chlorinated, which also weakens gluten |
| Pastry flour | 8-9% | Pie crust, tarts, biscuits |
| All-purpose | 10-12% | Genuinely all-purpose. Brand varies more than you would think |
| Bread flour | 12-14% | Bread, pizza, bagels |
| High-gluten | 14-15% | Bagels, very chewy pizza |
| Whole wheat | 13-15% listed | Misleading. Bran fragments cut the network. Behaves weaker |
| Semolina (durum) | 12-13% | Dry pasta, some breads. Coarse, very extensible |
| 00 flour | Varies, 8-12% | Defined by grind fineness, not protein. Check the bag |

Two of these mislead people.

**Whole wheat** lists high protein but produces weaker structure, because the
bran particles are physically sharp and sever gluten strands as the dough is
worked. It also absorbs far more water. Substituting whole wheat one-for-one gives
you a dense, dry result unless you increase hydration and expect less rise.

**00** is an Italian grind classification — how finely the flour is milled — not
a protein specification. There is 00 pasta flour and 00 pizza flour with quite
different protein contents. Read the bag rather than the number.

Regional note: European all-purpose flours generally run lower in protein than
American ones, which is why an American bread recipe made in France often comes
out slack, and a French pastry recipe made in the US comes out tough.

## The levers

Six things change how much network you end up with.

**Protein content.** Sets the ceiling. Nothing below can exceed it.

**Hydration.** Water is required for the proteins to move and bond at all. Too
little and the network cannot form. More water makes a slacker, more extensible
dough that develops more readily but holds shape less. Expressed as baker's
percentage — water weight over flour weight. Bread runs 60 to 80 percent, pasta
around 40 to 50, pastry 20 to 30.

**Mixing.** The direct input. Every stroke aligns and links more protein. This is
the lever you actually operate minute to minute, and the one people get wrong in
both directions — over-mixing muffins, under-kneading bread.

**Time.** Gluten develops on its own, slowly, without mechanical work. An
**autolyse** — mixing flour and water and leaving it 20 to 60 minutes before
anything else — does a meaningful share of the kneading for you. A no-knead bread
substitutes twelve hours for ten minutes of work.

**Salt.** Strengthens and tightens the network, and slows yeast. This is why bread
without salt is not merely bland but also slack and oddly textured. Add salt after
an autolyse rather than before, since it slows hydration.

**Fat and sugar.** Both interfere, on purpose. Fat coats protein and physically
blocks bonding, which is what "shortening" means — it shortens gluten strands.
This is why pastry is tender and why laminated doughs stay flaky. Sugar competes
for water, leaving less available for the proteins.

## Resting

A rested dough is a different dough, and skipping the rest is the most common
reason a dough "will not behave."

Two things happen during a rest. Water finishes penetrating the flour particles,
so hydration completes. And the gluten network **relaxes** — the bonds under
tension gradually rearrange into lower-energy configurations, so elasticity drops
and extensibility rises.

Practically: dough that springs back the instant you roll it is not badly made.
It is not rested. Cover it, wait 20 to 30 minutes, and it will roll out without
resistance. Fighting it instead is how you end up with tough pastry and a torn
sheet of pasta.

Rest pasta dough at least 30 minutes. Rest pastry in the fridge, which also
re-firms the fat. Rest bread between folds.

## The windowpane test

The standard check for whether bread dough is developed enough.

Tear off a piece, flatten it, and stretch it gently between your fingers. If you
can get it thin enough to see light through without tearing, the network is
strong and even. If it tears while still thick and opaque, keep going.

This works because a well-developed network is a continuous film. An
underdeveloped one is a collection of unconnected clumps, and it fails wherever
the connections are missing.

It is not appropriate for every dough — you will never windowpane a pastry, and
you should not want to.

## Non-wheat flours

They contain no glutenin or gliadin, so no network forms. Anything that relies on
structure needs it supplied another way — eggs, xanthan gum, psyllium, or a
different structural strategy entirely.

| Flour | Behavior | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rye | Very little gluten, high pentosans | Sticky, dense. Sourdough helps |
| Spelt | Some gluten, fragile | Develops fast, breaks down fast. Do not over-knead |
| Rice | None | Gritty unless finely milled. Good for coatings and frying |
| Almond and nut flours | None | High fat. Structure from eggs |
| Buckwheat | None, not a wheat at all | Strong flavor. Soba, galettes |
| Cornmeal and masa | None | Masa's structure is from nixtamalized starch |
| Chickpea | None | Binds well through its own protein. Socca, panisse |

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tough muffins, pancakes, scones | Over-mixed | Stop at just-combined. Lumps are fine |
| Tough pie crust | Over-worked, or too much water | Less water, less handling, keep the fat cold |
| Bread will not rise | Under-kneaded, or dead yeast | Windowpane test. Check the yeast separately |
| Dough springs back constantly | Not rested | Cover, wait 20-30 min. Do not fight it |
| Pasta tears when rolled | Too dry, or not rested | More water next time, rest this one |
| Bread dense and heavy | Low-protein flour, or whole wheat swapped in one-for-one | Bread flour, or raise hydration for whole wheat |
| Dough slack and unmanageable | Over-hydrated for that flour, or no salt | Less water, and do not skip salt |
| Whole wheat loaf is dry | Bran absorbs more water | Increase hydration by 5-10 percent |
| Gluten-free bread crumbles | No network and nothing replacing it | Binder - xanthan, psyllium, egg |
| Everything inconsistent batch to batch | Measuring flour by volume | Weigh it. See `found.measuring` |
