## Nothing happens until you cut

An intact garlic clove barely smells like garlic. An unpeeled onion does not make
you cry. Both are nearly inert until damaged, and that is not an accident — it is
a defense system that only arms on being eaten.

Allium cells store two things in separate compartments: sulfur-containing
compounds that are themselves odorless, and enzymes that act on them. Intact,
they never meet. Rupture the cell walls and they mix, and within seconds the
enzymes convert the odorless precursors into the sharp, volatile compounds you
recognize.

In garlic, the enzyme alliinase converts alliin into **allicin**, which is
responsible for most of raw garlic's pungency and its antimicrobial activity. In
onion, a parallel reaction produces, among other things, the volatile compound
that reaches your eye and makes it water.

Three consequences follow, and they are the practical core of this lesson.

**More rupture means more pungency.** Cut size is a dial. A whole clove simmered
in a sauce contributes background sweetness. Thin slices contribute noticeable
garlic. A fine mince contributes a lot. A clove crushed to paste with salt
contributes an aggressive amount, because the salt crystals act as an abrasive
and rupture nearly every cell.

| Preparation | Cells ruptured | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole, peeled | Almost none | Mild, sweet, background |
| Smashed, left in pieces | Some | Moderate, easy to fish out later |
| Sliced thin | Moderate | Clearly present, browns evenly |
| Minced fine | High | Strong, distributes everywhere, burns fastest |
| Pounded to paste with salt | Nearly all | Maximum. For raw applications |

**Timing matters.** The enzyme keeps working after the cut, so the pungency of
minced garlic climbs for a few minutes and then, if it sits, starts turning
harsh. Garlic minced twenty minutes before it hits the pan tastes different, and
worse, than garlic minced just before. Cut alliums close to when you use them.

**Heat stops the reaction.** The enzyme is a protein and denatures with heat. Add
whole cloves to already-hot oil and much less allicin ever forms, which is why
whole cloves confited in oil come out sweet and nutty rather than sharp. The
inverse is also true: acid slows the enzyme, so garlic minced directly into
lemon juice stays milder than garlic minced onto a dry board.

## Heat rewrites it again

Once cut, what happens next is a second, completely separate transformation.

Those sharp sulfur compounds are volatile — they evaporate and they break down
with heat. As they leave, what remains is the allium's substantial sugar content,
which then browns. So the arc of cooking an onion is: **sharpness leaves,
sweetness arrives, then browning flavors arrive.**

| Stage | Time (medium-low) | Looks like | Tastes like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Raw | 0 | White, crisp | Sharp, hot, aggressive |
| Softened | 2-3 min | Edges going clear | Still sharp, softer texture |
| Sweated | 5-8 min | Translucent, no color | Sweet, mild, no bite |
| Lightly golden | 10-15 min | Pale gold at the edges | Sweet with a little depth |
| Caramelized | 35-50 min | Deep brown, jammy, collapsed | Very sweet, complex, savory |
| Burnt | Any time, on high | Black flecks | Bitter. Unrecoverable |

The two named techniques on that arc:

**Sweating** is cooking in fat over low-to-medium heat, often with a pinch of
salt, deliberately *without* color. The goal is to remove sharpness and release
sweetness while keeping the flavor clean. Salt helps by drawing water out
osmotically, which keeps the surface wet enough to prevent browning — a case
where the water release that ruins a saute is exactly what you want. This is the
correct treatment for the base of most soups, sauces, and braises.

**Browning** is medium heat and more time, letting Maillard and caramelization
run. Deeper and more complex, but it commits the dish in a direction. A tomato
sauce built on sweated onion tastes clean and bright; the same sauce on browned
onion tastes deeper and less fresh. Neither is wrong, but you should pick.

Caramelized onions get their own lesson — `prep.caramelized-onions` — because the
main thing to learn about them is how long they actually take, which is roughly
four times what most recipes claim.

## The family

They are not interchangeable, and the differences are mostly sugar content, water
content, and how much of the sulfur precursor each carries.

| Allium | Character | Best at | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yellow onion | Balanced sugar and sulfur | The default. Cooking base for almost anything | Nothing. This is the workhorse |
| White onion | Sharper, less sweet, more water | Raw applications, Mexican cooking, salsa | Harsher raw than yellow |
| Red onion | Sharp raw, muddy cooked, anthocyanin color | Raw, pickled, grilled | Goes grey-blue when cooked in some liquids |
| Sweet onion (Vidalia, Walla Walla) | High water, low sulfur, not actually higher sugar | Raw, or quick cooking | Too watery for long cooking; will not caramelize well |
| Shallot | Finer, more delicate, more sugar | Pan sauces, vinaigrettes, anywhere subtlety matters | Burns faster than onion |
| Garlic | Dominant, high sugar | Everywhere, in controlled amounts | Burns fast and turns bitter |
| Leek | Mild, sweet, silky | Soups, braises, slow cooking | Grit between layers. Wash after cutting |
| Scallion | Two ingredients in one - sharp white, mild green | White with the aromatics, green as a finish | Do not cook the greens long |
| Chive | Barely allium, mostly fresh | Raw finish only | Heat destroys it entirely |

Leeks deserve a warning. Soil gets trapped between the layers as they grow, and
rinsing the outside does nothing. Cut them first, then swish the pieces in a bowl
of water and lift them out, leaving the grit behind. Do not pour through a
colander — that pours the grit back over them.

## Sequencing, and the garlic problem

The single most common aromatic failure in home kitchens is burnt garlic, and it
happens because garlic and onion get added together.

Garlic has more sugar and far less water than onion, and it is usually cut much
smaller. All three push in the same direction: it browns much faster, and shortly
after browning it burns. Burnt garlic is bitter, the bitterness distributes
through the fat, and there is no correction. You start over.

The order, then:

1. **Fat into the pan, then heat.** Or heat then fat — but see the exception
   below.
2. **Onion, shallot, leek, celery, carrot.** The slow, wet ones. Several minutes.
3. **Garlic, ginger, chile.** 30 to 60 seconds, until fragrant. Fragrant is the
   cue, not color.
4. **Ground spices.** 20 to 30 seconds. See `flavor.spices`.
5. **Liquid**, which stops all browning immediately.

The exception is deliberate and worth knowing: starting garlic in **cold** oil and
bringing them up together. The garlic heats slowly and evenly, infuses the oil
thoroughly, and browns gently rather than shocking. This is the right method for
garlic oil, for the base of `garlic-chile-oil-pasta`, and for anywhere you want
deep garlic flavor in the fat rather than garlic pieces in the dish.

## Choosing a treatment

| You want | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| Clean, bright base for a sauce | Sweat onion in fat with salt, no color, 6-8 min |
| Deep savory base | Brown the onion properly, 15-20 min |
| Sweet, jammy, almost a condiment | Caramelize, 40 min, low and slow |
| Garlic present but not loud | Whole or smashed cloves, removed later |
| Garlic forward | Sliced or minced, added late |
| Garlic aggressive, raw | Pounded to paste with salt |
| Garlic infused into fat | Cold-start in oil, bring up slowly |
| Raw onion in a salad without the harshness | Slice thin, soak in cold water 10 min, or cut with the grain |
| Onion visible in the finished braise | Cut with the grain, larger dice |
| Onion invisible, dissolved | Cut across the grain, small dice |

The last two are `knife.onion-dice`, and they are where cutting and flavor meet.

## What goes wrong

- **Garlic in with the onion.** The default error. Bitter, and unrecoverable.
- **Prepping garlic far ahead.** Pungency climbs and then goes harsh. Cut it
  close to use.
- **Sweating on too high a heat.** You wanted no color and now you have color and
  a committed dish.
- **Not enough time on the base.** Under-sweated onion carries its raw sharpness
  straight into the finished sauce, and it will still be there an hour later.
- **Unwashed leeks.** Grit in the finished dish, and nothing to do about it.
- **Sweet onions for a long braise.** Too much water, not enough sulfur to convert
  into depth.
- **Cooking chive or scallion greens.** They are finishing ingredients.
- **Crowding the aromatics.** They steam and never sweeten. See `heat.saute`.
