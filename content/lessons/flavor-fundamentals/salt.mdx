## Salt is not a flavor, it is an amplifier

The naive model of salt is that it adds saltiness, and that you add it until food
tastes salty enough. That model produces mediocre food and it does not explain
why properly salted food tastes *more like itself* rather than more like salt.

Four things are going on.

**It suppresses bitterness.** Sodium ions interfere with bitter receptors more
than with any other taste. Salting a bitter green, a grapefruit, or a cup of
coffee reduces the bitterness disproportionately, and the sweetness and aroma
underneath become audible. Much of what we perceive as salt "bringing out flavor"
is actually salt removing a competing signal.

**It releases aroma.** Salt changes how volatile aroma compounds distribute
between food and the air above it, driving more of them into the air where your
nose can reach them. Since most of what you experience as flavor is smell rather
than taste, this is a large effect.

**It moves water.** Salt draws water out of cells by osmosis and, given time,
carries itself back in. This is the mechanism behind brining, curing, drawing
water out of vegetables, and most of the texture effects below.

**It changes proteins.** Salt partially denatures muscle proteins, letting them
hold more water when cooked. Salted meat is measurably juicier than unsalted meat
cooked identically — but only if the salt has had time to get in.

That last conditional is the whole game.

## When to salt: the diffusion problem

Salt moves through food slowly. Not minutes — hours, for anything thick. Every
piece of salting advice is really a statement about how much time you are giving
diffusion.

**Salt on the surface, seconds before cooking,** is a garnish. It sits on top,
hits your tongue directly, and reads as sharply salty without seasoning anything
below the surface. This is a real technique — it is why flaky finishing salt
exists — but it is not seasoning the food.

**Salt with enough time to penetrate** dissolves, moves inward, and seasons
throughout. The food does not taste "salty"; it tastes complete.

Rough guidance:

| Food | Salt it | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thin cutlets, fish, shrimp | 10-15 minutes ahead, or immediately before | Thin enough that diffusion is quick; fish gets firm and dry if salted far ahead |
| Steaks, chops | Either 45+ minutes ahead, or immediately before cooking | See the awkward middle, below |
| Whole chicken, roasts | 12-24 hours ahead, uncovered in the fridge | Deep penetration plus a dried surface for browning |
| Ground meat for burgers | At the very last moment, on the outside only | Salt mixed in dissolves proteins and makes the texture springy and sausage-like |
| Vegetables for roasting | Just before, with the oil | You want browning, not water draw |
| Vegetables you want to drain (eggplant, cabbage, cucumber) | 30+ minutes ahead, then blot | Here the water draw is the entire point |
| Beans and legumes | From the start of cooking | The old "salt toughens beans" claim is largely wrong; salted beans cook fine and season through |
| Pasta water | Once boiling, generously | Only chance to season the pasta itself |
| Soups, stews, braises | In layers throughout, tasting each time | Long cook, plenty of diffusion time, and reduction concentrates what is there |

### The awkward middle

For a steak, there is a window between roughly two minutes and forty minutes
where the salt has drawn moisture to the surface but has not yet been reabsorbed.
The surface is wet, so it browns badly, and the interior is not seasoned yet. It
is the worst of both.

So either salt right before it hits the pan, or salt at least forty-five minutes
ahead — ideally hours. Do not land in the middle.

## Dry brining

The technique that follows from all of the above, and probably the highest-value
thing in this lesson.

Salt the surface of a protein, leave it **uncovered** on a rack in the
refrigerator for anywhere from an hour to a day, and cook it. That is all.

What happens in sequence:

1. Salt draws moisture out of the surface by osmosis.
2. The salt dissolves in that moisture, forming a concentrated brine on the
   surface.
3. That brine is reabsorbed, carrying salt into the meat.
4. Salt partially denatures the muscle proteins so they retain more water during
   cooking.
5. Meanwhile, the dry refrigerator air dehydrates the surface.

You get seasoning that goes all the way through, meat that holds moisture better
under heat, and a dry exterior that browns far better than a fresh one. Three
benefits, one step, no equipment.

**Dry brining beats wet brining** for most purposes. Wet brining adds water to the
meat, which dilutes flavor and actively harms browning. Wet brining still wins
where you want maximum moisture insurance on something very lean and easily
overcooked — a turkey breast for a crowd — but for weeknight cooking, dry is
simpler and better.

The Ragusea video linked above argues against salting meat in advance, on
grounds broadly opposite to this. His reasoning is not stupid and the effect
sizes here are genuinely debated. Watch it, then run the practice exercise and
decide for yourself. This is a good early instance of a case where the
"consensus" is thinner than it is usually presented.

## Salt types, and why teaspoons lie

This causes more oversalted dinners than any other single thing.

Salt crystals differ enormously in shape and density. Diamond Crystal kosher salt
is hollow and flaky; Morton kosher is denser and more compressed; table salt is
fine, dense cubes. A teaspoon of each contains very different amounts of sodium.

Approximate equivalences by volume:

| To replace | Table salt | Morton kosher | Diamond Crystal kosher |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 tsp table salt | 1 tsp | ~1.5 tsp | ~2 tsp |
| 1 tsp Morton kosher | ~2/3 tsp | 1 tsp | ~1.5 tsp |
| 1 tsp Diamond Crystal | ~1/2 tsp | ~2/3 tsp | 1 tsp |

Roughly a **2:1 spread** between table salt and Diamond Crystal. A recipe calling
for a tablespoon of Diamond Crystal, made with table salt, is double-salted and
inedible.

Two practical responses:

- **Weigh it** when precision matters — brines, cures, doughs. Grams are unit-
  agnostic and this problem disappears.
- **Pick one salt and stay with it** for everyday cooking. Your hand calibrates to
  a specific crystal within a few weeks and then you stop measuring at all. Most
  cooks land on Diamond Crystal because the large flakes are easy to pinch and
  forgiving to over-pour.

Keep it in an open bowl, not a shaker. Pinching gives you a physical sense of
quantity that shaking never will, and it makes small increments natural.

**Finishing salts** — Maldon, fleur de sel — are a separate category. Large,
crunchy flakes added at the last second, for texture and for discrete bursts of
salinity. Do not cook with them; you are paying for a crystal structure that
dissolves away.

**Iodized versus not** rarely matters for flavor, though some people detect a
slight metallic note. Iodine in salt exists for public-health reasons and that is
a genuine consideration if it is your main dietary source.

## Salting in layers

The last principle, and the one that separates deep food from flat food.

Salting each component as it enters — the aromatics as they sweat, the vegetables
as they go in, the liquid when it is added — produces a fundamentally different
result from adding the same total amount at the end. Each component gets seasoned
from the inside, and the layers stack.

End-salting produces a dish where salt sits on top of unseasoned food. It reads
as salty *and* bland simultaneously, which is a confusing and common failure.

The check: salt a little at each stage, taste at each stage, and remember that
you can always add more.

## The failure modes

- **Salting only at the end.** Salty on the surface, bland underneath.
- **Salting in the awkward middle** before a sear. Wet surface, no browning, no
  penetration.
- **Switching salt brands without adjusting.** Up to double the sodium for the
  same measurement.
- **Salting a reduction early.** Reduction concentrates salt. Season at the end.
- **Forgetting the salty components still to come.** Anchovy, soy, capers, olives,
  cured meat, parmesan, and stock all bring their own.
- **Salting when the dish needs acid.** If it keeps wanting more salt and never
  arrives, stop and add lemon or vinegar instead. This is the single most useful
  correction in home cooking.
