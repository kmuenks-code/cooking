## The taste you have been calling something else

Sweet, sour, salty, bitter — those four are old news, and umami joined them
relatively recently as a matter of Western acceptance. It was identified in 1908
by Kikunae Ikeda, working out what made kombu broth taste the way it did, and it
took most of a century and the discovery of the actual receptors before the
category stopped being controversial.

You already know the taste. It is what makes parmesan, soy sauce, anchovy, aged
beef, ripe tomato, mushroom, fish sauce, and good stock all feel like they share
something despite tasting nothing alike. That shared thing is savoriness — a
mouth-filling, lingering quality that reads as *depth* rather than as a flavor.

Its practical importance is diagnostic. When a dish tastes flat, most home cooks
reach for salt. Sometimes salt is right. But when a dish is correctly salted and
still feels thin, adding more salt makes it salty *and* thin, and the cook
concludes they are bad at seasoning. What was missing was a different axis.

**Salt makes food taste more like itself. Umami makes food taste like more.**

## The chemistry, briefly

Two families of compounds, and the interaction between them is the part worth
knowing.

**Glutamates.** Free glutamic acid — an amino acid — unbound from protein. The
main one is monosodium glutamate, which occurs naturally in kombu, tomato,
parmesan, soy sauce, mushroom, and anything aged or fermented.

**Nucleotides.** Chiefly inosinate (IMP) and guanylate (GMP). Inosinate comes
from animal tissue: dried bonito, cured pork, anchovy, meat, fish. Guanylate
comes mainly from dried mushrooms — dried shiitake in particular has a great deal
of it, and fresh shiitake has far less.

Alone, each produces umami. Together, the effect is not additive — it is
**multiplicative**. Combining a glutamate source with a nucleotide source
produces an umami intensity several times stronger than the sum of the two,
because the nucleotide increases the receptor's sensitivity to glutamate.

This is not a curiosity. It is the explanation for a large number of classic
combinations that were arrived at empirically, centuries before anyone knew why:

- **Dashi** — kombu (glutamate) plus katsuobushi (inosinate). Two ingredients,
  and the reason is exactly this.
- **Parmesan on tomato sauce** — glutamate on glutamate, but the ageing in the
  cheese also produces nucleotides.
- **Anchovy melted into a tomato base** — inosinate meeting glutamate.
- **Mushrooms with beef** — guanylate meeting inosinate and glutamate.
- **Bacon with tomato.**
- **Fish sauce in almost anything Southeast Asian.**
- **Soy sauce with dried shiitake.**

The operational rule that falls out of this: **when you want depth, use two
sources from different families rather than more of one.** A teaspoon of tomato
paste and one anchovy will do more than three teaspoons of tomato paste.

## Where umami comes from

Free glutamate is created when bound protein breaks down. Anything that breaks
down protein over time increases umami, which is why these four processes all
produce savory ingredients:

- **Ageing** — dry-aged beef, aged cheese, cured ham.
- **Drying** — sun-dried tomato, dried mushroom, dried fish. Concentration plus
  enzymatic breakdown.
- **Fermentation** — soy sauce, miso, fish sauce, kimchi. Microbes doing the
  protein breakdown for you.
- **Long slow cooking** — stock, braise, ragu. Hours of heat hydrolyzing protein.

This is a useful lens. A ragu simmered for four hours has more umami than the
same ingredients simmered for one, and not only because it is more reduced.

## The pantry shortlist

| Source | Family | Adds | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tomato paste | Glutamate | Sweetness, color, body | Nearly universal. Bloom in fat first |
| Anchovy | Inosinate | Salt and depth, no fish flavor if melted | Tomato sauce, braises, dressings, greens |
| Fish sauce | Inosinate | Big depth, funk if overused | Start with half a teaspoon in anything savory |
| Soy sauce | Glutamate | Salt, color, depth | Braises, marinades, anything already dark |
| Parmesan and its rind | Both | Nutty savory depth | Rind into any simmering pot |
| Dried shiitake | Guanylate | Deep earthy savoriness | Soak, use mushroom and liquid both |
| Miso | Glutamate | Depth and body, plus salt | Whisk into liquids off the heat |
| Kombu | Glutamate | Clean, clear savoriness | Cold-steep for stock. Never boil it |
| Worcestershire | Both | Anchovy, tamarind, spice, all at once | The lazy option, and it works |
| Marmite or yeast extract | Glutamate | Enormous depth, distinctive | Tiny amounts in stews and gravies |
| Cured pork | Inosinate | Fat, salt, depth | Render at the start of a base |
| MSG | Glutamate | Pure umami, nothing else | When you want depth and no new flavor |
| Mushroom powder | Guanylate | Depth, no texture | Ground dried mushrooms. Keep a jar |

**Choose by what else the ingredient brings.** Every one of these carries flavor
beyond umami. Fish sauce brings funk. Soy brings color and its own fermented
character. Parmesan brings dairy. If you want savoriness without changing the
dish's identity, the neutral options are MSG, mushroom powder, and a melted
anchovy — anchovy dissolves completely in warm fat and, in small amounts, is not
detectable as fish. It is the reason a lot of restaurant vegetable dishes taste
better than yours.

## The MSG question

This deserves to be stated plainly rather than danced around.

Monosodium glutamate is the sodium salt of glutamic acid. It is chemically
identical to the free glutamate in parmesan, tomato, and soy sauce. Your body
cannot distinguish the source, and glutamate from a shaker and glutamate from a
tomato are metabolized the same way.

The reputation traces to a 1968 letter to a medical journal coining "Chinese
Restaurant Syndrome," which was never a controlled finding. Subsequent
double-blind studies have consistently failed to reproduce the reported effects
at normal dietary levels. Major food safety bodies classify it as safe.

There is a real caveat worth stating: a small number of people report sensitivity
at large doses on an empty stomach, and that self-report is not nothing even
where the mechanism is unclear. But at the quantities used in cooking — a
quarter-teaspoon in a pot — this is not a meaningful concern, and the same person
will happily eat parmesan, which delivers more glutamate per bite.

Use it or do not. But if you avoid it while cooking with soy sauce and parmesan
and tomato paste, you are avoiding a word rather than a molecule.

## Technique notes

**Bloom tomato paste.** Add it to the fat after the aromatics and cook it,
stirring, for 30 to 60 seconds until it darkens from bright red to brick and
sticks slightly. This caramelizes its sugars and drives off the tinny raw note.
Enormous return on half a minute, and skipping it is why some tomato sauces taste
faintly of can.

**Melt anchovy, do not add it.** Into warm fat, mashed with a spoon, until it
disappears entirely. A visible piece of anchovy tastes like anchovy. A dissolved
one tastes like the dish is better.

**Never boil kombu.** Above roughly 140F it releases bitter compounds and a
slippery texture. Cold-steep it, or bring it just to the edge of a simmer and
pull it out.

**Add miso off the heat.** Boiling drives off its aromatic complexity and can
make it grainy. Whisk it into a little of the hot liquid first, then stir back.

**Salt and umami interact.** Glutamate makes food taste more savory, and it also
makes it taste somewhat saltier. Add umami first, then reassess salt. You will
often need less.

## What goes wrong

- **Reaching for salt when the issue is depth.** The core error. You end up with
  a salty flat dish.
- **One source, more of it.** Two families multiply. One family just accumulates.
- **Undissolved anchovy.** Now the dish tastes of fish.
- **Too much fish sauce.** The margin between depth and funk is narrow. Half a
  teaspoon, taste, repeat.
- **Skipping the tomato paste bloom.** Raw metallic note straight into the sauce.
- **Boiled kombu.** Bitter and slimy.
- **Boiled miso.** Flat and grainy.
- **Salting before adding umami.** You will overshoot the salt.
- **Adding umami to a dish that needs acid.** Depth on top of richness reads as
  heavy. Check `flavor.acid` before assuming the answer is more savoriness.
