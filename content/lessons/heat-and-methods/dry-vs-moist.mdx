## One number explains most of this

At sea level, water boils at 212F. Add more heat and it does not get hotter — the
energy goes into converting liquid into vapor rather than into raising the
temperature. That is latent heat of vaporization, and it is why a pot at a
furious rolling boil is exactly the same temperature as one barely simmering.

Now the second number. The Maillard reaction — the browning that produces the
flavor of seared steak, toasted bread, and roasted onion — proceeds far too
slowly to matter below about 250F, and gets going properly somewhere around 300
to 330F. Caramelization of sugars starts higher still, above 320F for most.

**212 is below 300.** So as long as there is liquid water sitting on the surface
of your food, that surface cannot reach browning temperature. It is not slow. It
is not a matter of patience. It is off the table entirely.

Every distinction in this lesson comes from that one gap.

## The two families

| | Dry heat | Moist heat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medium touching the food | Air, fat, or radiation | Water or steam |
| Surface temperature ceiling | As high as you like | 212F |
| Browning | Yes | Never |
| Flavor development | New compounds created | Existing flavors extracted or diluted |
| Texture at the surface | Crust, crisp | Soft, yielding |
| Collagen conversion | Poor, dries out first | Excellent |
| Typical failure | Burnt outside, raw inside | Bland and grey |

**Dry heat**: roasting, baking, searing, sauteing, grilling, broiling, pan-frying,
deep frying. Note that frying is dry heat — fat is not water, it goes well past
212F, and it browns. The word "dry" is about the absence of water, not the
absence of liquid.

**Moist heat**: boiling, simmering, poaching, steaming, blanching, and the
covered phase of a braise or a stew.

## What each one is good at

Dry heat *creates* flavor. Maillard and caramelization take amino acids and
sugars that were already in the food and build hundreds of aromatic compounds
that were not there before. A roasted onion does not taste like a more intense
raw onion. It tastes like a different thing, because it chemically is one.

Moist heat *moves* flavor. It cannot create browning compounds, so what it does
instead is transfer: it dissolves soluble flavor out of ingredients and into
liquid, or carries seasoning from a liquid into food. That is a real capability
and not a consolation prize — it is the whole basis of stock, and of anything
poached in a flavored liquid.

Moist heat is also better at two structural jobs. It transfers heat far more
efficiently than air does, so it cooks quickly and evenly at a low temperature —
which is exactly what a delicate protein wants. And it converts collagen to
gelatin without drying out the meat, because the environment is saturated. That
second one is why a tough cut becomes tender in a braise and merely becomes tough
and dry in an oven.

## Why efficiency differs so much

A 200F oven will not cook you. A 200F pot of water will injure you badly and
fast. Same temperature.

The difference is how quickly each medium hands over energy. Water is dense and
conducts far better than air, so it delivers heat to a surface roughly twenty
times faster. Steam is better still at the moment it condenses, because
condensation releases the whole latent heat of vaporization directly onto the
food.

Consequences worth holding onto:

- A 200F poach cooks faster than a 200F oven, by a lot.
- Boiling water is unforgiving in a way a low oven is not. Seconds matter.
- A convection oven — moving air — closes some of this gap, which is why it
  cooks faster at the same set temperature.
- An oven's dial is a poor guide to how fast food will actually cook, because
  air is such a bad conductor. This is why oven recipes have such wide time
  ranges.

## Combination methods

The two families are not mutually exclusive within a dish, and the best methods
use both deliberately in sequence.

A **braise** sears first — dry heat, for browning and flavor creation — then adds
liquid and covers — moist heat, for collagen conversion. Reverse the order and
you get neither: a wet surface will not brown, and you have wasted the sear.

A **stew** is the same logic at smaller piece size. A **pot roast** is a braise
with a bigger cut. **Roasting a chicken over vegetables** is dry heat above and
moist heat below, which is why the underside skin is never crisp.

The rule, and it is nearly universal: **brown first, then add liquid.** Once the
liquid is in, the browning window has closed for good.

## The trap: dry methods that go moist

Here is the part that catches people, and it is worth more than the taxonomy
above.

A method being dry-heat by category does not guarantee a dry surface in practice.
**Food brings its own water.** Vegetables are mostly water. Meat is around
seventy percent water. As it heats, that water comes out.

If it evaporates as fast as it emerges, the surface stays dry and browns. If it
accumulates faster than it can leave, the surface is now sitting in water, the
temperature there is pinned at 212F, and you are steaming — inside a skillet, on
purpose, in a pan you believed was searing.

This is the single most common failure in home cooking. It is why:

- Crowded pans produce grey, floppy mushrooms instead of browned ones.
- A pan of vegetables straight from a rinse gives up water before it browns.
- Meat that went in wet from the package sits and hisses and never crusts.
- A cold pan lets water out before the surface is hot enough to drive it off.
- A lid on a "searing" pan guarantees no sear, because it traps the vapor.

What keeps a dry method dry:

- **Enough pan area per piece.** Room for vapor to escape.
- **A dry starting surface.** Pat it, or salt it ahead and let it dry.
- **A hot enough pan.** Water hitting a hot surface leaves. Water hitting a warm
  one pools.
- **No lid**, unless you have deliberately switched to moist heat.
- **Batches, not one big load.** Two rounds of browned is better than one round
  of steamed.

`heat.saute` is largely about managing exactly this, and `heat.searing-maillard`
covers the browning itself.

## Choosing between them

| You want | Family | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crust, deep savory flavor | Dry | Sear, roast, saute, fry |
| Tender tough cut | Moist, after a dry sear | Braise, stew |
| Delicate protein, no browning | Moist | Poach, steam |
| Vegetable color and crispness preserved | Moist, briefly | Blanch and shock |
| Vegetable sweetened and browned | Dry | Roast |
| Flavor pulled out into a liquid | Moist | Simmer for stock |
| Flavor pushed into food | Moist | Poach in a seasoned liquid |
| Even edge-to-edge doneness | Moist, low | Poach, or sous vide |

## What goes wrong

- **Expecting browning from a moist method.** No amount of extra time gets you
  there. Physics, not patience.
- **Adding liquid before browning.** The window is closed permanently.
- **Crowding, and calling the result a saute.** Grey, wet, and disappointing.
- **Wet food into a dry-heat pan.** Dry the surface first.
- **A lid on a browning pan.** Traps the vapor you are trying to lose.
- **Boiling a tough cut instead of simmering it.** Moist heat is right, but hard
  boiling shreds muscle fiber while the collagen is still converting.
- **Roasting a delicate fish.** The method will overcook and dry it long before
  it browns.
- **Reading the oven dial as a rate.** Air is a poor conductor. The number tells
  you a ceiling, not a speed.
