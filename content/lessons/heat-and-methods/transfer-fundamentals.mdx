## Why bother with the physics

You do not need this lesson to cook a decent dinner. You need it to stop
memorizing methods.

There are perhaps fifteen named cooking methods, and they look like fifteen
separate things to learn. They are not. They are combinations of three ways of
moving energy, applied through different media, and once you see that, roasting
and grilling and broiling stop being three facts and become one idea with the
knobs set differently.

More usefully: it lets you predict. Handed an unfamiliar method or an unfamiliar
piece of equipment, you can reason about what it will do rather than looking it
up.

## The three mechanisms

### Conduction — direct contact

Energy moves through direct physical contact, molecule to molecule. A pan touches
a steak; the steak's surface heats. Inside the food, conduction is also how heat
travels from the hot surface toward the cold center.

- **Fast at the point of contact**, because contact is intimate and continuous.
- **Very uneven**, because only the touching surface gets it.
- **Produces browning readily**, because the contact surface can far exceed 212F.

Pan-searing, griddling, and cooking on any solid hot surface are conduction
dominant. So, importantly, is the movement of heat *inside* any piece of food,
regardless of the method that put energy on its surface.

Food is a poor conductor. That is not a detail — it is the central problem of
cooking. It is why the outside of a thick steak is overcooked before the middle
is warm, and why every technique for a thick piece of protein is fundamentally a
strategy for managing that gradient.

### Convection — a moving fluid

Energy is carried by a moving fluid, either a gas (air, steam) or a liquid (water,
oil). The fluid touches the food, gives up heat, moves away, and is replaced by
more hot fluid.

- **Even**, because the fluid surrounds the food.
- **Speed depends enormously on the fluid.** Water conducts heat roughly 25 times
  better than air at the same temperature. This is why 212F water cooks food
  quickly while a 212F oven barely does anything, and why you can put your hand
  in a 350F oven for a second but not in 350F oil.
- **Speed also depends on how fast the fluid moves.** Still air next to food forms
  an insulating boundary layer. Moving air strips it away.

That last point is the entire explanation of a convection oven. Same temperature,
moving air, faster cooking — because the fan removes the stagnant boundary layer
that was insulating the food. This is why convection recipes tell you to lower
the temperature by 25F or shorten the time. Nothing changed except air movement.

It is also why a windy 35F day feels colder than a still one, which is a useful
thing to have in mind — wind chill and convection ovens are the same physics.

### Radiation — electromagnetic waves

Energy travels as infrared radiation, needing no contact and no medium. A broiler
element, glowing coals, and the walls of a hot oven all radiate.

- **Surface only.** Infrared penetrates food barely at all. It heats the surface
  and everything deeper happens by conduction from there.
- **Intense and fast**, and it falls off sharply with distance.
- **Excellent for browning**, terrible for cooking anything through.

Broiling, grilling over coals, and the browning contribution of an oven's hot
walls are radiation. It is the reason a grilled steak develops a crust in
conditions where the air alone could never do it.

## Real methods are blends

Almost nothing is purely one mechanism. Naming the blend is the useful skill:

| Method | Dominant | Also present |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pan-searing | Conduction from the pan | A little radiation from the pan surface |
| Deep frying | Convection through hot oil | Conduction where food touches the basket |
| Boiling and simmering | Convection through water | — |
| Steaming | Convection through steam, plus condensation | — |
| Roasting | Convection through air | Significant radiation from oven walls |
| Convection roasting | Forced convection | Radiation, and a thinner boundary layer |
| Grilling | Radiation from coals or burners | Conduction from the grates, convection from rising air |
| Broiling | Radiation, almost entirely | Minor convection |
| Braising | Convection through liquid | Conduction from the pot |
| Microwaving | None of the three | Dielectric heating, a genuinely different mechanism |

Microwaves are worth the aside. They excite water molecules directly throughout
the food's outer centimeter or so, rather than heating the surface and waiting
for conduction. That is why microwaves are fast, why they heat unevenly (they
follow water distribution, not geometry), and why they never brown — the surface
never gets hotter than the water in it, which caps out at boiling.

## Steam is the exception that explains a lot

Steam looks like it should behave like hot air. It does not, and the reason is
worth carrying.

When steam touches food cooler than 212F, it condenses back into liquid water.
That phase change releases a large amount of stored energy — the latent heat of
vaporization — directly into the food's surface. Enormously more energy than
simply cooling hot air by the same number of degrees.

This is why steam at 212F cooks far faster than air at 212F, and why a steam burn
is much worse than a hot-air burn at the same temperature. It is also why a wet
oven browns poorly: condensing steam keeps the surface at 212F, which is below
browning territory.

## Two consequences worth internalizing

**Thickness dominates cooking time, not weight.** Since interior cooking happens
by conduction through a poor conductor, the time for heat to reach the center
scales roughly with the *square* of the distance it has to travel. Double the
thickness and you roughly quadruple the time. A flattened cutlet and a thick chop
of identical weight are completely different cooking problems, and this is why
pounding a chicken breast thin is a legitimate technique rather than a cheat.

**Surface browning and interior doneness are separate problems with a shared
budget.** Browning requires a surface above roughly 300F. Interior doneness for
most proteins is 130-165F. The hotter and faster you drive the surface, the
steeper the gradient and the more overcooked the layer under the crust will be.

Every advanced protein technique is an attempt to decouple these. Reverse searing
(cook the interior gently first, then brown fast at the end) and sous vide (hold
the interior at exactly its target, then sear) are both explicitly the same
strategy: bring the interior up under shallow-gradient conditions, then apply an
intense, brief surface treatment. If that struck you as fussy before, it should
now look like the obvious solution to a problem you can name.

## The failure modes

- **Assuming oven temperature equals food temperature.** A 400F oven does not
  make food 400F. It supplies energy at a rate; the food's temperature depends on
  time, mass, and surface.
- **Ignoring the boundary layer.** Crowding a sheet pan traps moist air around
  the food, killing convection and browning. Space is not aesthetic.
- **Trying to brown in a wet environment.** Covered pans, crowded pans, and wet
  food surfaces all hold the surface at 212F, where browning cannot happen.
- **Using intense radiation to cook something through.** The broiler is a
  finishing tool. It will burn a surface long before the center moves.
- **Scaling time by weight.** Measure the thickness.
