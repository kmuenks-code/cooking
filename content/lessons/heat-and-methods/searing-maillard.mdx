## The myth first, because it is in the way

You have been told that searing seals in the juices.

It does not. This was proposed by Justus von Liebig in the 1850s, spread through
a century of cookbooks, and has been repeatedly disproven by the straightforward
experiment of weighing meat before and after. Seared meat loses *more* moisture
than unseared meat, not less, because you have applied intense heat to it. There
is no seal. Crust is porous.

This matters beyond trivia, because the myth leads people to sear defensively —
briefly, at the start, to "lock things in" — rather than treating browning as
the flavor-generating step it actually is.

**What searing actually does is create flavor that did not previously exist.**
Hundreds of new aromatic compounds, none of which were in the raw ingredient. It
is one of the largest single flavor contributions available in cooking, and it is
worth doing properly and for its own sake.

## What the Maillard reaction is

At its simplest: **amino acids react with reducing sugars under heat.** The
initial product rearranges and fragments into an enormous cascade of new
molecules — hundreds of distinct compounds, many of them intensely aromatic.

This is why browned food smells so different from raw food. It is not the same
flavor intensified. It is a genuinely different set of molecules, produced by a
reaction that only happens under specific conditions.

It is also why seared beef, toasted bread, roasted coffee, and browned onions all
share a family resemblance in aroma despite having nothing else in common. The
same reaction is running in all four, on different substrates.

**Maillard is not caramelization.** They are frequently conflated and they are
different reactions:

| | Maillard | Caramelization |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reactants | Amino acids **and** sugars | Sugars alone |
| Onset | Around 285-300F / 140-150C | 320F+ / 160C+, depending on the sugar |
| Where you see it | Meat, bread crust, roasted coffee, seared vegetables | Caramel, deeply browned onions, roasted sugar-heavy vegetables |
| Aroma character | Savory, meaty, roasted, complex | Sweet, nutty, slightly bitter |

Both are often running at once. Deeply browned onions are doing both — they have
sugars and amino acids and plenty of time.

## The four conditions

Browning requires all four. Miss one and you get grey food.

### 1. A dry surface

This is the one people underestimate, and it is the most common cause of failure.

Water boils at 212F. As long as there is liquid water on the surface of your
food, that surface **cannot exceed 212F**, because all incoming energy goes into
the phase change instead of raising temperature. Maillard needs roughly 300F. So
a wet surface is not merely slower to brown — it is physically prevented from
browning until every bit of surface water is gone.

Worse, boiling off that water consumes a large amount of energy, which pulls the
pan's temperature down at exactly the moment you need it high.

So: **pat it dry.** Paper towels, ten seconds. For a serious crust, leave the
protein uncovered on a rack in the fridge for a few hours or overnight — the dry
air dehydrates the surface far more thoroughly than a towel can, which is a large
part of why dry-brined meat browns so well.

### 2. Enough heat

Surface needs to reach roughly 300F, and in practice you want the pan
considerably hotter than that so the surface gets there quickly and the interior
does not overcook waiting.

This is the water-bead test from the heat control lesson. A skittering Leidenfrost
bead means you are there.

### 3. Space

Crowding kills browning through two mechanisms at once.

**Thermal:** every additional cold item draws more energy out of a pan with a
finite amount stored. Past a threshold the pan cannot recover and the surface
temperature stays below browning.

**Atmospheric:** food releases steam as it cooks. In a crowded pan that steam has
nowhere to go and forms a humid layer around the food, holding the surface at
212F. You are now steaming in a skillet.

The tell is audible. A proper sear is a loud, sustained sizzle from the moment
the food lands. If it goes quiet, or sounds wet and sputtery rather than sharp,
you are steaming. Cook in two batches. It is genuinely faster than fixing one bad
batch, which cannot be fixed.

### 4. Time, undisturbed

Browning is a reaction that proceeds over time. Moving food around interrupts
contact and resets the surface temperature.

Put it down and leave it. And here is the useful part: **the food will tell you
when it is ready.** Proteins bond chemically to bare metal on contact and release
when the surface has browned and dehydrated enough for those bonds to break down.

So the rule that solves most sticking complaints: **if it will not lift, it is not
done.** Forcing it tears the surface and leaves the crust in the pan. Wait another
thirty seconds and it usually releases cleanly, whole.

## An assist: alkalinity and sugar

Two levers, useful to know:

- **Maillard runs faster in alkaline conditions.** This is why pretzels are dipped
  in lye and why a pinch of baking soda in an onion pan dramatically accelerates
  browning. Used carelessly it produces a soapy taste and mushy texture, so it is
  a small pinch, not a spoonful.
- **The reaction needs sugar, and some foods have very little.** Very lean, very
  fresh proteins can brown reluctantly. A light dusting of sugar, or an
  ingredient that brings its own, can help. This is part of why marinades
  containing honey, soy, or fruit juice brown so aggressively — and why they burn
  so readily.

## Fond, and why this lesson is a prerequisite for sauces

After searing in a stainless or carbon steel pan, the bottom is covered in dark
brown stuck-on material. That is **fond**, and it is the point.

Fond is concentrated Maillard product — the same reaction that browned your food,
running on the drippings that landed on the pan surface and dehydrated there. It
is one of the most flavor-dense substances you can produce, and it is sitting in
your pan, fully soluble in liquid, ready to be lifted with a splash of wine or
stock.

That is the entire premise of a pan sauce, and it is why the sauce lesson lists
this one as a prerequisite. It is also the strongest practical argument for
searing in stainless rather than nonstick — nonstick will not give you fond at
all, because nothing sticks, which is the whole idea.

Do not wash that pan. It is worth more than what you just cooked in it.

## Diagnosing grey food

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pale and grey, lots of liquid in the pan | Wet surface, or crowded, or both | Dry the food, cook in batches |
| Browns but sticks and tears | Not enough preheat, or moved too early | Preheat properly, wait for release |
| Outside dark, inside raw | Pan too hot for the thickness | Lower heat, or sear then finish in the oven |
| Brown but tastes bitter | Past Maillard into burning | Lower heat; check whether the fat is smoking |
| Sizzle dies within seconds of adding food | Thin pan, too much cold food | Fewer pieces, heavier pan, more preheat |
| Uniformly brown but no crust | Steaming that eventually dried out | Higher heat from the start |

## The failure modes

- **Searing straight from the fridge without drying.** The single most common
  reason home-cooked protein is grey.
- **Crowding to save a batch's worth of time.** Costs you the crust and the fond.
- **Poking, flipping, and repositioning.** Every move resets the contact surface.
- **Searing in nonstick.** No fond, and coatings should not see that heat anyway.
- **Deglazing with cold liquid into a screaming-hot empty pan.** It will spit
  violently. Pull the heat back first.
- **Washing the pan.** You just threw away the sauce.
