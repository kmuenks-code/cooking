## What the word means

*Sauter* is French for "to jump," and the name describes the motion: small pieces
of food in a hot pan with a little fat, kept moving so they cook fast and evenly
without any one face sitting long enough to burn.

That is the technique. The interesting part is why it fails, because it fails in
one specific way and knowing that way is most of the skill.

## The evaporation budget

From `heat.dry-vs-moist`: as long as liquid water sits on the surface of food,
that surface is pinned at 212F, and browning does not begin in earnest until
around 300F.

Food releases water when it heats. Vegetables are largely water. Mushrooms are
about ninety percent water. Meat is around seventy.

So a hot pan is running a budget with two sides:

- **Water in**, from the food, as it heats.
- **Water out**, evaporated from the pan surface into the air.

**Out exceeds in:** the surface stays dry, the temperature climbs past 300F, and
you brown.

**In exceeds out:** water pools, the pan temperature crashes to 212F and stays
there, and you steam. Grey, floppy, and tasting of not much.

Every rule below is a way of managing one side of that budget.

### What sets water out

- **Pan surface area not covered by food.** Exposed hot metal is where
  evaporation actually happens. This is the biggest single factor.
- **Pan temperature.** Hotter drives water off faster.
- **Thermal mass.** A heavy pan holds its temperature when cold food lands. A
  thin one drops and takes a long time to recover.
- **Airflow.** A lid stops evaporation completely. So does a crowded pan with
  nowhere for vapor to go.

### What sets water in

- **Total mass of food.** More food, more water.
- **Water content of that food.** Mushrooms and zucchini are the hard cases.
- **Cut size.** More cut surface releases water faster.
- **Salt timing.** Salt draws water out osmotically. Salting vegetables at the
  start of a saute is a common own goal — it accelerates water release at exactly
  the wrong moment. Salt after browning has begun.
- **Surface wetness on arrival.** Rinsed and undried vegetables start the budget
  in deficit.

## Reading the pan

You do not need a thermometer. The pan tells you.

| Sound and sight | What it means | Do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loud, sharp sizzle | Water leaving fast. Browning | Nothing. It is working |
| Sizzle quiets to a murmur | Rate falling. Approaching the tipping point | Spread food out, raise heat |
| Visible steam clouding up | Steaming now | Raise heat, or pull half the food out |
| Liquid pooling | Steaming, badly | Remove half. Let the pan recover |
| Silence | Pan cooled below evaporation | Recovery unlikely. Restart in batches |
| Smoke, acrid | Fat past smoke point, or fond burning | Off heat. Start over if it smells bitter |

The loud sizzle is the sound of your method working. When it drops, you have
about thirty seconds to intervene before the pan is committed.

## Batch size

**Size the batch to the pan, not the pan to the batch.**

Working rule: food should cover roughly **two thirds** of the pan bottom, in a
single layer, with visible metal between pieces. Not a heap. Not a full carpet.

The instinct to fit everything in one pan is understandable — two batches is more
work and more time. But two batches of browned vegetables take about twelve
minutes and taste like something. One crowded batch takes eighteen, because the
pan spends the first ten minutes boiling off water, and tastes like a vegetable
that has been boiled and then briefly fried.

If you have too much food and only one pan, either brown in batches and combine,
or use a bigger pan. A twelve-inch skillet has roughly forty percent more area
than a ten-inch one, which is often exactly the margin you need.

## Sloped sides

A true saute pan has flared, sloping sides, and the reason is mechanical: the
slope lets you flick food up and have it land back in the pan instead of on the
stove. That is the tossing motion the name refers to.

The slope has a second effect that matters more. Flared sides increase the
opening relative to the base, which gives vapor an easier escape. A straight-
sided pan holds a small pocket of humid air over the food.

Straight-sided pans are better when you plan to add liquid later — a pan sauce, a
braise started in the same pan — because they hold more without slopping. For
pure sauteing, sloped is better.

Cast iron works but is slow to respond. Nonstick works but cannot get as hot and
gives you no fond, so it is the wrong choice if a pan sauce is coming. Stainless
or carbon steel over a heavy base is the default.

## Stir, or leave it alone

The name says keep it moving. The name is not always right.

**Keep it moving when:**

- Pieces are small and would burn on one face.
- Aromatics are involved — garlic goes from fragrant to acrid in under a minute.
- The pan is very hot.
- You want even cooking without deep browning, as in a stir-fry.

**Leave it alone when:**

- You want real browning on a flat face. Contact time is what builds Maillard,
  and stirring interrupts it. Mushrooms, onion for caramelizing, cut vegetables
  you want colored on one side.
- The pan has just received cold food and needs to recover.
- Food is stuck. Browned food releases itself. Forcing it early tears the crust
  off, leaves it in the pan, and gives you a wet face where a browned one was.

Practical compromise for most vegetables: leave undisturbed for two to three
minutes to establish color on one face, then stir every thirty to sixty seconds.
You are alternating contact time with redistribution.

## Sequencing a mixed pan

Different ingredients need different times, so put them in at different times.
Ordered longest-cooking first:

1. **Dense vegetables** — carrot, potato, winter squash. 6 to 10 minutes.
2. **Medium** — onion, celery, peppers, broccoli stems. 4 to 6 minutes.
3. **Quick** — zucchini, mushrooms (see below), snap peas, broccoli florets. 2 to
   4 minutes.
4. **Aromatics** — garlic, ginger, chile, ground spice. 30 to 60 seconds,
   near the end.
5. **Delicate greens** — spinach, chard leaves. Under a minute.
6. **Off-heat additions** — fresh herbs, acid, finishing fat, butter.

The one that catches everyone is **garlic**. It has a very high sugar content
relative to other aromatics, so it browns fast and then burns, and burnt garlic
is bitter in a way that cannot be corrected. It goes in near the end, not with
the onion, unless it is going into cold fat that you are bringing up slowly on
purpose. `flavor.aromatics-alliums` covers this properly.

**Mushrooms** deserve a note. They hold so much water that a normal saute floods
the pan. Three approaches, all legitimate: give them a very large pan and high
heat and let the water rip off; cook them in a dry pan with no fat at all until
the water has left, then add fat and brown; or accept the flood, cook until the
liquid boils away, and then start browning in what is left. The second is the
most reliable and the most counterintuitive.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grey, wet, no color | Crowded | Two thirds coverage. Batches |
| Liquid in the pan | Water release outran evaporation | Remove half, raise heat, let it recover |
| Vegetables released water immediately | Salted at the start | Salt after browning starts |
| Food stuck and tore | Moved before the crust formed | Wait. It releases itself |
| Burnt outside, raw inside | Heat too high for the piece size | Lower heat, or cut smaller |
| Bitter | Burnt garlic or burnt fond | Not fixable. Add garlic later |
| Fat smoking immediately | Wrong fat for the temperature | Higher smoke point, or lower heat |
| Everything mushy | Cut too small, or cooked too long after water release | Bigger cut, shorter cook |
| Some done, some raw | Uneven cuts | See `knife.uniform-cuts` |
| Pan never got hot again | Thin pan, too much cold food | Heavier pan, smaller batches |
