## Boiling is not a temperature

The most useful thing in this lesson: **turning the burner up under a boiling pot
does not make it hotter.**

Once water reaches 212F at sea level, additional energy goes into converting
liquid to steam, not into raising temperature. A pot at maximum heat and a pot
barely holding a boil are both at 212F. What you gain by cranking the dial is
bubbles — more vapor produced per second — and bubbles are mechanical agitation,
not heat.

So the dial on a pot of water is not a temperature control. It is an **agitation
control.** That reframing is what makes the rest of this straightforward.

There is one exception that matters. If you are trying to *reduce* a liquid, more
vigorous boiling genuinely does help, because you are trying to drive off water
and a harder boil drives it off faster. Reduction is the case where the extra
energy is doing your work rather than shaking your food apart. See
`sauce.reduction`.

## The stages, and what each is for

You will not have a thermometer in the pot, so learn to read it.

| Stage | Roughly | What you see | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shiver | 140-160F | Surface trembles, no bubbles | Delicate poaching - fish, eggs |
| Sub-simmer | 160-180F | Small bubbles at the bottom, few reaching the top | Stock, custards, confit-adjacent |
| Gentle simmer | 180-200F | Steady stream of small bubbles breaking the surface | Braises, beans, most long cooking |
| Boil | 212F | Constant bubbles across the whole surface | Pasta, green vegetables, potatoes |
| Rolling boil | 212F | Violent, cannot be stirred down | Blanching, reduction, starting pasta |

Two of these deserve attention.

**Stock lives at sub-simmer, and this is not fussiness.** Above about 180F, a
hard bubble action emulsifies rendered fat into the liquid and mechanically
breaks up proteins and vegetable solids, and both of those turn the stock cloudy
permanently. There is no filtering it back out. A stock held below a simmer for
hours stays clear. `prep.stock` covers this.

**"Bring to a boil, then reduce to a simmer"** is a standard recipe line and it
is a real instruction, not a formality. Boiling gets you to temperature fast.
Simmering is where the food should actually sit.

## Why agitation matters so much

Consider what a rolling boil physically does to something suspended in it.
Bubbles form on the bottom, expand, rise, and collapse, and the resulting
currents throw food against the sides of the pot and against itself, repeatedly,
for the whole cook.

For a potato that is fine. For an egg in the shell, fine. For anything with
fragile structure, it is destructive:

- **Fish** falls apart into flakes.
- **Poached eggs** shed their whites into ragged streamers.
- **Beans** lose their skins and break down into a mash.
- **Braised meat** shreds along its grain before the collagen has finished
  converting, which is why a boiled braise is both stringy and dry.
- **Dumplings and gnocchi** disintegrate.
- **Stock** goes cloudy.

The general rule: **if the food has structure you want to keep, do not boil it.**
Simmer or poach instead. Same temperature, minus the violence.

## When you actually want a boil

Boiling is right when agitation helps or does not hurt.

- **Pasta.** Movement keeps strands from sticking to each other, and pasta is
  robust. A rolling boil is the correct call.
- **Green vegetables.** Speed is the whole strategy, and a hard boil recovers
  temperature fast after the cold vegetables go in. See blanching below.
- **Potatoes and root vegetables.** Dense and sturdy. Though starting potatoes in
  *cold* water and bringing them up together cooks them more evenly, since a
  large potato dropped into boiling water overcooks its outside while the middle
  catches up.
- **Reduction.** The one case where the extra energy is the point.
- **Getting to temperature.** Boil hard, then back off.

## Blanching

Blanching is a brief boil followed immediately by an ice bath. It is not a way
of cooking something through. It is a **timing and preparation tool**, and the
two halves do different jobs.

**The boil** does three things: it deactivates the enzymes that would otherwise
degrade color and texture over the following days, it drives dissolved gases out
of the spaces between plant cells, and it partially cooks the vegetable.

That second one is the reason blanched green vegetables go so startlingly bright.
The tiny gas pockets in raw plant tissue scatter light and dull the color you see.
Drive them out and the chlorophyll underneath shows through. It is an optical
change, and it happens within seconds.

**The shock** stops the cooking dead. Vegetables carry a lot of residual heat, and
a vegetable pulled to a colander keeps cooking for minutes — long enough to go
from perfect to olive-drab while you are doing something else. Ice water ends it.

The color loss you are racing is a genuine chemical change: prolonged heat, and
especially heat plus the acids the vegetable itself releases, strips the magnesium
atom out of the chlorophyll molecule and replaces it with hydrogen, producing a
dull olive-brown compound. That reaction is irreversible. It is why boiled-to-
death green beans never come back, and why the window is around three minutes,
not ten.

### Doing it

1. **Salt the water heavily.** Heavier than pasta water. This is a short contact
   time and it is the only chance the vegetable gets to be seasoned from within.
2. **Rolling boil, and a lot of water.** Cold vegetables drop the temperature.
   Volume is what lets it recover fast.
3. **Ice bath ready before the vegetables go in.** Actual ice, not cold tap
   water. There is no time to make it later.
4. **Work in batches** if the pot cannot recover its boil quickly.
5. **Pull while still firm.** Green beans, 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Broccoli
   florets, 2 to 3. Asparagus, 1 to 2. Spinach, 20 seconds. Peas, 60 seconds.
6. **Straight into the ice, submerged.** Not a colander first.
7. **Pull once cold, and dry thoroughly.** Wet blanched vegetables will steam
   rather than brown if they later meet a hot pan, and will water down any
   dressing.

### Why bother

- **Do the work early.** Blanch in the afternoon, finish in ninety seconds at
  dinner. This is the single biggest reason restaurants blanch.
- **Get everything to finish at once.** Blanch the dense vegetables, then combine
  everything for a brief final cook.
- **Freeze without ruin.** Unblanched frozen vegetables degrade because their
  enzymes keep working at freezer temperatures.
- **Loosen skins.** Thirty seconds gets peels off tomatoes and peaches cleanly.
- **Take the edge off** raw onion or garlic without cooking it.

## Salting the water

Water dissolves and carries away soluble compounds from whatever is in it. That
includes the food's own salts and sugars — which means unsalted cooking water
actively pulls flavor out.

Salting the water reduces that gradient and gets seasoning into the interior in a
way that surface salting afterward cannot match.

- **Pasta water**: about 1 to 2 percent salt by weight of water, which is roughly
  10 to 20 grams per liter. The frequently repeated "as salty as the sea" is
  around 3.5 percent and is genuinely too much for most sauces. Start at 1
  percent and taste the water — it should taste seasoned, not painful.
- **Vegetable water**: as salty or slightly more. Short contact time.
- **Blanching water**: heavily salted.
- **Stock**: do not salt. It is going to be reduced later, possibly by a lot, and
  salting now decides the seasoning of every dish it eventually goes into. See
  `sauce.reduction`.
- **Potatoes for mashing**: salt it. It is the only way to get salt into the
  center.

Salt does raise water's boiling point, but at these concentrations the effect is
a fraction of a degree. Anyone telling you to salt the water so it boils hotter
is wrong. It also does not meaningfully affect how fast the water comes to a
boil.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Braise came out stringy and dry | Boiled instead of simmered | Bare simmer, lid ajar. Same temperature, less violence |
| Stock is cloudy | Boiled | Sub-simmer, and never stir it |
| Poached fish fell apart | Water too active | Shiver, not simmer |
| Beans lost their skins | Rolling boil | Gentle simmer from the start |
| Green vegetables went olive | Cooked too long, or never shocked | Shorter boil, real ice bath |
| Blanched vegetables taste of nothing | Water was not salted | Heavily salt the water |
| Pasta gluey and stuck | Not enough water, or not enough movement | More water, real boil, stir at the start |
| Potato mushy outside, hard middle | Dropped into already-boiling water | Start dense roots in cold water |
| Sauce too salty later | Salted the stock before reducing | Never salt anything you intend to reduce |
