## Three jobs, not one

Most people think of the oil in a pan as anti-stick. That is the least
interesting thing it does.

**Job one: it fills the gaps.** At a microscopic level, both your pan and your
food are rough. When you put a chicken thigh in a dry pan, the actual metal-to-
food contact is a scatter of high points, and the spaces between are filled with
air — one of the best insulators available. The result is a few scorched contact
points and a lot of surface that is barely heating at all.

A thin film of fat floods those gaps with a liquid that conducts heat perfectly
well. Contact goes from patchy to complete, and the surface heats evenly. This is
why even a nonstick pan cooks better with a little fat in it, despite not needing
it to prevent sticking.

**Job two: it can get hot enough to brown.** Water caps out at 212F. Browning
starts meaningfully around 300F. Any water-based medium is therefore permanently
locked out of browning — this is the single fact that divides the cooking methods
into those that can brown and those that cannot. Fat has no such limit in the
range we cook at. Oil at 375F is unremarkable.

**Job three: it dissolves things water cannot.** A large fraction of the aroma
compounds in spices, chiles, and aromatics are fat-soluble and not water-soluble.
Bloom cumin in oil and the oil turns fragrant and yellow. Stir the same cumin
into a simmering broth and most of what you paid for stays locked in the seed.
This is why so many cuisines start by cooking spices in fat, and it gets its own
lesson later in Flavor Fundamentals.

## Smoke point, and what is actually burning

Every fat has a temperature at which it begins to break down visibly. Past that
point, triglycerides decompose — releasing free fatty acids, and eventually
acrolein, which is what makes overheated oil smell acrid and taste bitter.

Two things determine where that point sits:

- **Refinement.** Refining strips out free fatty acids and particulates. Refined
  oils have substantially higher smoke points than unrefined versions of the same
  oil.
- **Impurities.** Anything suspended in the fat burns before the fat does. This
  is why butter smokes early — the milk solids scorch long before the butterfat
  itself is in trouble — and why oil that has already fried something is dirtier
  and smokes sooner.

Approximate values, and treat them as approximate. Published numbers vary widely
between sources because they depend on refinement and freshness:

| Fat | Rough smoke point | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Butter | 300F / 150C | The milk solids, not the fat, are the limit |
| Extra virgin olive oil | 325-375F / 165-190C | Higher than folklore claims, and the flavor degrades before the smoke point |
| Coconut oil (virgin) | 350F / 175C | Distinct flavor |
| Lard, tallow | 370-400F / 190-205C | Excellent flavor, good stability |
| Refined olive or "light" olive oil | 400-465F / 205-240C | Neutral, none of the good flavor |
| Canola, vegetable, sunflower | 400-450F / 205-230C | The workhorse neutrals |
| Clarified butter / ghee | 450F / 230C | Butter flavor without the milk solids |
| Avocado oil (refined) | 480-520F / 250-270C | Highest common option, expensive |

## Choosing a fat

Two questions, in order: **how hot am I going, and do I want to taste it?**

| Situation | Use | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hard sear, high heat | Neutral high-smoke-point oil, or ghee, or tallow | You are above butter's ceiling from the start |
| Sauteing aromatics at medium | Butter, olive oil, or either | Well below any relevant limit; pick for flavor |
| Deep frying | Refined neutral oil | High smoke point, no flavor of its own, cost matters at volume |
| Finishing a dish off the heat | Extra virgin olive oil, butter, a nut oil | Flavor is the entire point and there is no heat to degrade it |
| Anything that ends in a pan sauce | Neutral oil to sear, butter at the end | The oil takes the heat, the butter brings flavor and gloss when it is safe |
| Baking | Whatever the recipe specifies | Fat is structural here, not a medium. Substituting changes texture |

That fifth row is the most useful habit in this lesson: **sear in oil, finish in
butter.** You get butter's flavor without asking it to survive temperatures that
will scorch its milk solids. Nearly every restaurant pan sauce works this way.

## Butter, in detail

Butter is roughly 80% fat, 16-18% water, and 1-2% milk solids, and it behaves
like three ingredients because it is three ingredients.

Watch a pan of butter and it narrates its own state:

1. **Melting** — nothing interesting yet.
2. **Foaming loudly** — the water content is boiling off. Loud foam means water
   is still present, so the pan cannot exceed 212F yet. Food added now will not
   brown.
3. **Foam subsides** — the water is gone. Now the pan can climb, and browning
   becomes possible. This is the moment to add food for most sauteing.
4. **Milk solids turning golden and smelling nutty** — this is *beurre noisette*,
   brown butter, and it is a legitimate destination in its own right. Toasted
   milk proteins are the flavor.
5. **Solids blackening, sharp acrid smell** — gone. Start over.

The water content also explains why butter alone is poor for a hard sear: you
have to boil off 16% of it before the pan can even reach browning temperature,
by which point the solids are close to burning.

**Clarified butter and ghee** remove the water and the milk solids, leaving
nearly pure butterfat. Smoke point jumps to around 450F, and you keep most of the
butter flavor. Worth making if you sear often.

**Adding oil to butter does not meaningfully raise the smoke point.** This is a
widely repeated piece of kitchen folklore and it is basically wrong — the milk
solids are still in the pan and still burn at the same temperature. What the oil
actually does is dilute the solids and improve heat distribution, so scorching is
less concentrated and less likely to catch. The practice is fine. The usual
explanation for it is not.

## The failure modes

- **Not enough fat.** A dry pan leaves air gaps and you get patchy contact,
  scorched spots, and sticking. A thin film is doing real work.
- **Too much fat for a saute.** Food ends up shallow-frying and turns greasy.
  Enough to coat, not enough to pool.
- **Adding food during the foam.** The pan cannot brown while water is boiling
  off.
- **Searing in butter or extra virgin olive oil.** You will be at the smoke point
  before the pan is at searing temperature.
- **Reusing oil that has already smoked.** Its smoke point is now lower and the
  off flavors carry into whatever you cook next.
- **Spending good olive oil on high heat.** Its distinctive flavor compounds are
  volatile and degrade well before the smoke point. If you are going to heat it
  hard, use the cheap refined one and save the good bottle for finishing.
